Context

The ticket: “the detail meant for an implementer LLM and the summary that I need to read in order to approve a spec are all mixed together.” It asks for a standard HTML format split into an above-the-fold brief (“key decisions, questions, UI demos, architecture diagrams — skimmable, the smallest possible amount of info”) and a below-the-fold detail for the AI. It also asks for a visual language that combines mid-century print principles (from the plate-book reference the owner attached), a 19th-century British feel (the serif wordmark) and alfred’s own glow-and-flat look. This spec is itself written in the proposed format, as the first test of its budgets.

Decision records

D1The look of the brief · open

Thesis. Mid-century print, the Victorian jobbing printer and alfred share one belief: a limited kit, spent on structure. The Victorian printer had a case of type and one or two inks. The mid-century designer paid per plate. Alfred has a navy ground, one light accent and a glow. So this is one rule in two settings, not a blend of styles:

  • By day the document prints: navy and teal ink on cool paper, with a hard teal offset (misregistration) where a shadow would go.
  • By night it glows: alfred’s own dark tokens, where the offset becomes alfred’s teal glow. Misregistration and glow are the same idea, a second colour displaced from the first, printed subtractively on paper and additively on a screen.
  • Each day ink pairs with alfred’s accent in the same hue (teal↔teal, oxblood↔red, brass↔amber).
  • The app mockup is a colour plate tipped into a Victorian book, captioned “Plate I.”

Plate-book rules taken:

  • The ink budget (paper plus three inks, tints via color-mix, no new hues).
  • Paper is never #fff and ink is never #000.
  • Scale contrast of at least 4:1, carried by the numeral (about 6:1).
  • Radius is 0 or 999px in the chrome.
  • Hard, unblurred offsets.
  • Texture on chrome only.
  • Every mark encodes something.
  • Flush left.
  • One display face, used three or four times.
  • CSS-only :has() switching.

Rejected:

  • Overprint/multiply: a spec has nothing to overlap.
  • Ink starvation, displacement and halftone: bytes and effect.
  • A full-page grain overlay: it sits over reading text.
  • Webfont CDNs (see D6).
  • Uppercase tracked labels: the most common tell of a generated page.

Inside a plate, the app keeps its own radii. The hard-cornered document next to the round-cornered product helps tell the two apart.

On your reply: fold the picked options into the template’s base CSS as the only look. Delete every other option, all dial markup, CSS and script, and the html:has(#…) rules. The losing options must not survive in the template.

D2What sits above the fold · proposed

The brief holds three kinds of thing: what changes (one sentence), the calls the human must see, and a drawing of everything they’ll see. The section list, order and budgets are in §3. The budget is at most 300 words of prose for a story (450 for an epic), not counting plates and figures, which is about a minute of reading plus the pictures. Rebuilt this way, ALF-268’s brief is about 130 words (a 22-word change, four one-line rows, one plate, its existing before/after figure, two heads-up lines).

Kept out of the brief:

  • Context/problem: the human wrote the ticket, and a misread shows up just as clearly in #change.
  • A scope-in list: redundant with #change and the decisions.
  • An acceptance-criteria count: it carries no decision.
  • Implementation risks.
  • A status line such as “2 open · 3 proposed”: a second copy of the list that drifts.

Alternatives:

  • A TL;DR on top of today’s document: it drifts, and still buries the mockup.
  • Collapsing the detail: see D9.
  • Two files: the Worker snapshots one file, and the implementer needs both halves.

“Above the fold” is a metaphor. On a phone, a UI spec’s brief is still several screens, because the plates can’t be shrunk under the sign-off rule. So the budget is on prose, not pixels, and the ids are brief and detail.

D3Which calls reach you · proposed

A decision appears in the brief only if at least one of these holds:

  • (a) the human would see its result;
  • (b) it is costly to reverse: schema, external contract, data loss, money, or a new service or dependency;
  • (c) it departs from the ticket, the epic or a prior spec.

Every other call is an implementation decision, recorded below only. Refinements:

  • A purely visual call with no credible alternative gets no row; its plate is the sign-off.
  • A choice the ticket already pinned goes in #change, not a row.
  • Settled rows carry the pick only, with no why.

The cap is 7 rows for a story and 12 for an epic. Going over is the “Not a clean one-story spec? Say so.” signal the skill already asks for. The cap will bite specs of ALF-261’s size (12 decisions, 11.7k words); that is intended, so don’t loosen it on the first collision. Applying this filter roughly halves ALF-161’s and ALF-162’s decision lists.

D4How you answer · proposed

Every row carries data-state:

  • open: needs the human’s pick. List the options, mark the recommendation with ★, and use the same letters in the plate’s switcher. It is for a genuine taste call, not a check the session skipped.
  • proposed: the session’s call. Merging accepts it.
  • settled: the human decided, in the session or in review.

The human answers in a PR comment by id (D2: B, D3 ✗ — do X instead). Silence means accept. An open row must not survive to merge; if the human approves with one open, the session says so in its PR reply. The revision flow is in §3.

Alternative rejected: a standing “Questions for you” section. The recent evidence says it is almost always “None”, and an empty section trains the reader to skip it. What is left in today’s open-question sections is implementer unknowns (“verify on a real iPhone”), and those move to #out.

D5Keeping brief and detail in step · proposed

Each fact lives once. The brief states the pick. The detail explains and elaborates it by id (D2, P1) and never restates or contradicts it. On conflict the brief wins, because it is what the human approved; for anything visual, the plate wins over prose. Every acceptance criterion traces up with data-trace="D#/P#" or impl. A criterion that changes what the human sees but traces to nothing above was never signed off: promote it to a row or plate, or cut it.
  • Brief row href="#dN" points to the record section#dN. Plates are figure#pN.
  • State lives only in the brief row’s data-state. Records repeat it in their heading for reading convenience, and the row wins.
  • To change a pick on revision, edit the row and its record in the same commit (grep dN).
  • This generalizes the skill’s existing “a visual requirement the mockup doesn’t draw isn’t settled” rule from pictures to all behaviour.

D6Fonts · proposed

The document chrome uses system stacks only (see §4). Every face in them ships on macOS and iOS, where the owner reads specs, and each stack degrades to an intentional-looking fallback elsewhere.

The one webfont is the app’s own font, for mockups only. Mockups name Geist, but Geist isn’t a system font, so every spec mockup so far has rendered in SF Pro on the owner’s phone. That quietly breaks the skill’s “fonts from the app’s stylesheet” rule. The fix adds nothing to the repo:

  • frontend/public/fonts/geist-sans.woff2 (28 KB, variable) is already committed.
  • raw.githubusercontent.com serves it with access-control-allow-origin: * (verified). It comes back as application/octet-stream with nosniff, which browsers accept for fonts.
  • The template’s plate section tells the session to load the app’s own committed font by its raw URL when that font isn’t a system face. It falls back to system-ui offline, or if the repo goes private.

Alternatives:

  • Base64-embedding a font: rejected. It adds about 30–70 KB to every spec, which the implementer reads first because it sits in <head>.
  • A relative URL to a committed font: rejected. It breaks at the epics/ and archive/ depths, and in the app’s single-file snapshot.
  • A committed Google face: allowed by the owner. Libre Caslon Display (the 1844 Chiswick Press Caslon revival) was considered for the numeral and title. It doesn’t beat Apple’s Baskerville on the owner’s devices, and would only help Android and Windows readers.

D7Scope and delivery · settled

Settled in session:

  • Story specs and epic specs adopt the format. Epic specs get it through the reference they already make to refinement’s mechanics.
  • Spike findings don’t, because they are a different genre that already leads with the answer.
  • Delivery is a committed template at .claude/skills/refinement/assets/spec-template.html, plus edits to the existing refinement skill (no new skill), plus the epic-refinement pointer.
  • The setup README must copy the whole skill folder, or the template never reaches project repos.

Implementation decisions

D8 · Decisions are a stacked list, not a table

A five-column table (id, question, pick, state, why) breaks at 390px. The stacked entry keeps the pick as the largest line, puts the state in the heading, and hangs the mark in the gutter.

D9 · No <details> collapse of the detail

The implementer reads the source anyway. Collapsing hurts find-in-page and makes #dN jumps inconsistent across browsers. A short brief and a visible fold do the job.

D10 · The template ships one look

No dials and no runtime toggle: every CSS byte is paid in every spec, forever. The edition follows prefers-color-scheme unless D1 picks a single edition.

D11 · Static first

The brief, plates and switchers render with scripting off, including inside the app’s sandbox="" view. Switchers are radio inputs with CSS selecting on :has(:checked), as this page’s dials are. Script is enhancement only, such as “Copy my picks”.

D12 · State renders from data-state

The hanging mark, the state word in each row heading, and the masthead stamp (“Your call needed” when any row is open, “Ready for review” otherwise) are all CSS reading the attribute. They can’t disagree with it, and flipping a row is a one-attribute edit.

D13 · Placeholders, not samples

The template uses [bracketed] placeholders and guide: comments that carry each section’s required/optional status and budget. It has no worked example, because a worked example gets copied verbatim and its fake content leaks into real specs. This page is the worked example.

D14 · The masthead imprint

The alfred wordmark appears on every project’s specs, as a publisher’s imprint: alfred’s pipeline produces the document, so the project name sits beside “Refinement spec” or “Epic spec”. Otherwise the template stays project-agnostic: no app paths, token values or refs.

Degenerate cases

  • Tiny spec (one file, like ALF-68): h1, #change and one plate. No row survives D3. Below: #context (2–3 sentences), #plan, #ac, #tests, #demo.
  • Non-UI spec: no plate unless something human-visible changes (CLI output, notification text, a written file), in which case a verbatim sample is the plate. The brief is #change, the costly rows, #diagram if a flow changes, and #heads-up.
  • Only decision pinned by the ticket: no rows. The choice is stated in #change, and the plate still draws it.
  • Everything settled in session: every row is settled and pick-only. The review becomes “did you record what I said”.

A review round

  1. In session: ask first (the existing rule). Answers become settled rows.
  2. Writing: the session’s own calls that pass D3 become proposed. A genuine taste call becomes open, with options A/B, ★ on the recommendation, and a plate switcher labelled D3: A | B.
  3. Review: the human answers by id in PR comments. Silence accepts.
  4. Revision, in the same round, for each answered id:
    • flip the row to settled and rewrite the pick;
    • move the chosen alternative up in the record, and reduce the others to one “considered” line;
    • re-rest the plate on the chosen option and delete the losing options (ALF-270 kept them live, which invites building the wrong one);
    • sweep the behaviour, criteria and captions that hung on the old pick (the epic skill’s stale-drawing rule, now for stories too);
    • replace #revised.
  5. Merge = approval of every proposed row. No open row survives it.
