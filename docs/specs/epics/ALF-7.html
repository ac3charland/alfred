ALF-7 — Communication Firewall Module

Epic spec Durable context for the epic. Read this before refining any story inside it.

The one-line shape. A new Comms module aggregates four accounts — two Gmail, one Amazon WorkMail, and iMessage — and shows the owner one question: what do I owe someone a reply to? A small model reads each arriving message against a hand-written rubric and a list of important people, and assigns it to one of four tiers. Only messages that ask something of the owner are queued; everything else falls to an unbadged FYI shelf. alfred never becomes the store of record — every message stays where it already lives, and the module is a view over it.

1 · Problem space & why now

SPEC.md has named this module since the beginning. §13.2 describes it as "an LLM with access to email / messaging … triages incoming messages by priority and urgency … presented in a dedicated triage UI", §5.1 reserves it a top-level tab, and §5.2 reserves the (firewall) route group. The accent is already assigned: Firewall = blue (--color-accent-blue, #60a5fa).

What the owner is actually buying

This is the part that shapes every decision below, and it is not what the SPEC's one-line description implies.

The prior art for this epic is Steve Newman's "Radar", demonstrated on the Cognitive Revolution podcast (transcript committed at docs/communication-firewall/). Steve's return on it was volume-driven: a few hundred messages a day across five apps, checked around thirty times a day. The firewall bought back hours.

The owner's volume is roughly 15 texts and 30 emails on a heavy day — an order of magnitude less. At that volume the time saved is maybe fifteen minutes, and a rubric hand-tuned exception by exception is a real recurring cost against it. Time is not the win. In the owner's words:

What it does buy is clarity and fewer items in my working memory — it lets me abstract "what do I need to respond to" to the single bit of "check comms". I'm afraid of (continuing to) put off or miss important communications because I get overwhelmed by triaging all of it. I'm fixing an avoidance pattern.

Three things follow, and they are the reason this epic looks different from the SPEC's sketch:

  • The product is a response queue, not a unified inbox. A message that asks nothing of the owner has no business in a queue whose whole purpose is answering "do I owe anyone anything."
  • Zero is the mechanism. "Check comms" only collapses to one bit if the answer is usually nothing. The counts are the product; the list is the fallback.
  • A false negative is the only failure that matters. One genuinely missed message doesn't cost that message — it costs trust, and the owner resumes checking Gmail "just in case", leaving two inboxes and a worse problem than before.

What this epic builds on

  • ALF-158 (LLM processing) — the Inbox classifier established the pattern this epic reuses: a cron'd Cloudflare Worker sweep, a small model, an enum'd structured verdict, a service-role write, and a per-failure degradation table. The prompt and the correction loop are Inbox-specific and are not reused (see D11).
  • The iMessage ingestion spike — settles that iMessage has no cloud API, that chat.db polling on a Mac is the only viable read path, and how to do it without the reliability gremlins.
  • The outbound-notifications spike — already chose a private Telegram bot, sent from the existing Worker, as alfred's way of reaching the owner. It explicitly anticipated this module as the consumer.

2 · Decisions

What the module is

D1Comms is its own module with its own tables. A message is never an item.

New route group, new tab, new Supabase tables. Messages do not enter the generic-item core, and nothing auto-converts a message into a task or a code item.

Why: the lifecycles don't match. An item is captured, classified, filed and completed; a message arrives, is answered elsewhere, and is never "done" in alfred's sense. SPEC sizes the item core for hundreds of active items — email volume would drown the Inbox and break every existing count and view. The owner also wants spin-off to be a deliberate act: "If tasks/code spring off from communications, I'll do that manually."

D2alfred mirrors. It is never the store of record.

Every message stays in Gmail or Messages.app. alfred holds a copy, a verdict, and triage state. Losing alfred's database loses the interface and nothing else.

Why: this is the load-bearing property of Steve's system and the reason he can ship to production with no staging — "all my messages really live where they've always been… if this totally falls down, everything is still where it's always been. I just lose the nice interface to it." It also means retention and deletion in alfred are safe by construction.

D3Only messages that ask something of the owner enter the queue.

Obligation is the entry ticket, not a sort key. A message that needs no reply is never queued — it lands on the FYI shelf (D6).

Why: the premise is avoidance, not time. The bit the owner wants is "do I owe anyone anything", and a queue containing no-action mail cannot answer it. Non-actionable mail is already handled fine in Gmail and Messages directly.

D4Four tiers: ASAP / Today / Whenever / FYI.

TierMeaningBadge
ASAPBreak focus for it. Someone is blocked on the owner, it is time-critical, or the sender is important enough that delay costs something real.Counted
TodayNeeds a reply before the owner logs off; not worth interrupting for. Most real correspondence.Counted
WheneverA reply is genuinely owed, but nothing turns on the date.Counted
FYIEverything else. No reply owed.None — collapsed by default

Why the shape diverges from Steve's: for Steve, the bottom tier is largely the junk pile. Here junk never enters the queue at all, so Whenever means owed but undated — which is precisely the three-week-avoidance bucket. It has to stay visible rather than being where things go to die.

D5One blended priority, with obligation as a floor.

The model returns a single tier, not a grid. Whether the owner owes a reply is an input to that one number — but an obligation never falls below Whenever.

Why: the owner asked for one number to glance at, and a 2×2 urgency × obligation grid gives four. But a pure urgency sort buries the non-urgent-but-owed message at the bottom — reintroducing the exact failure the epic exists to fix. The floor rule keeps one number while making that class of message structurally un-buriable.

D6FYI is a safety net, not a bin — and everything is stored.

Every ingested message is persisted and searchable regardless of tier. FYI is an unbadged, collapsed shelf the owner can open to spot-check the rubric.

Why: once only obligations are queued (D3), a misjudged message is invisible rather than merely buried — a strictly worse failure mode. FYI is what keeps a false negative recoverable, and it is the surface on which the rubric's recall can actually be audited. Unbadged so it reads as an archive, not a to-do.

D7Recall over precision, deliberately.

The rubric is tuned to over-surface. False negatives are the only metric that counts in the evaluation fixtures; false positives are a rounding error.

Why: at ~45 messages a day, three spurious ASAPs cost the owner seconds. One missed obligation costs the module's entire reason to exist. The asymmetry is extreme and the tuning should reflect it.

Ingestion

D8The Mac daemon is a dumb pipe. All triage runs in the Worker.

The daemon polls, normalizes, and POSTs the full message to an authenticated alfred ingest endpoint. It contains no model call and holds no Anthropic key.

Why: one classifier and one rubric across all four accounts. The alternative — triaging locally and shipping only a verdict — means two rubrics that drift apart, a second Anthropic key on a laptop when wrangler.toml states the Worker's is "the only place in the whole system that holds one", and a Comms view that can't show the message it's asking about. Accepted cost: personal iMessage bodies, including other people's words, are copied into Supabase. Single-user project, RLS on, the owner's own instance — weighed and accepted.

D9Two ingestion homes: the Worker for Gmail, the Mac for iMessage and WorkMail.

SourceHomeMechanism
Gmail ×2 (personal + one RealPlay)Cloudflare WorkerGmail API, one OAuth refresh token each, polled on the existing cron
Amazon WorkMailMac daemonIMAP
iMessage / SMSMac daemon~/Library/Messages/chat.db, read-only

Why: iMessage has no cloud API of any kind, so the daemon must exist regardless. WorkMail is neither Gmail nor Microsoft Graph — it is Amazon's Exchange-compatible service, and its practical read path is IMAP. No IMAP client is known to run in the Workers runtime (Workers can open raw TCP via cloudflare:sockets, but nothing in this repo has tried it, and most Node IMAP libraries assume net/tls). Since the daemon exists anyway, WorkMail rides along at near-zero marginal cost. Accepted cost: the Mac is a single point of failure for two of four sources.

D10The daemon polls. It does not watch the filesystem.

Poll on last-seen ROWID, every few seconds, with a read-only SQLite connection. Wrapped in a launchd agent with a health check that fails loudly.

Why: straight from the spike. macOS coalesces FSEvents for background processes it considers idle, so a pure watcher silently stalls for minutes on a quiet machine — exactly the condition under which a firewall must keep working. Read-only avoids contention with Messages.app, which holds the write lock. The loud health check exists because Full Disk Access grants have been observed to reset silently across macOS updates, and a firewall that fails quietly is worse than one that fails.

D11The rubric is a hand-edited document, not a learned correction loop.

A versioned prose document, editable in alfred, injected into every prompt. When the model gets something wrong, the owner adds an exception — the same way Steve accumulated "about a one-page rubric, gradually, exception by exception."

Why — and this is a deliberate divergence from ALF-158: the Inbox classifier records every override and feeds corrections back as few-shot examples. Urgency is policy, not pattern. "Anything from my wife is ASAP" should be true immediately and inspectably, not something the owner hopes thirty examples eventually teach. A learned loop also can't be audited when it gets something wrong, and auditability is what buys the trust this module runs on.

D12The people list is structured data, keyed on a person.

A contacts table — a person, their priority, and their many handles (a phone number, three email addresses). Rendered into the prompt; edited in the UI, not in prose.

Why: "these people are important" is a lookup, not a judgment. Keeping it out of the prose rubric makes it editable from a row, auditable ("flagged because it's from Dana"), and immune to the drift a paragraph suffers. Keying on the person rather than the address is forced by the multi-channel design: the same human is a phone number in iMessage and an email address in two mailboxes.

D13Newsletters never reach the classifier.

Detected deterministically from headers — List-Unsubscribe, List-ID, Precedence: bulk — before any model call, and filed straight to FYI. They are reserved for the future Reader module (SPEC §13.1).

Why: bulk mail is always non-urgent and never an obligation, so a model call to decide that is pure waste. Steve runs Radar and his RSS reader as separate tools for the same reason. Headers answer it for free, and keeping the whale out of the prompt keeps both the counts and the bill honest.

The verdict

D14The verdict states the ask, not the topic.

The per-message summary answers "what does this want from me, and by when" — not "what is this about". "Dana needs the invoice approved before Friday", never "regarding the Q3 invoice".

Why: what makes triage aversive is ambiguity about whether a message contains an obligation. A topic summary preserves that ambiguity and forces the owner to open the message to resolve it — which is the exact work the module is supposed to remove.

D15Haiku 4.5 to start, chosen by evaluation rather than by price. One call per message.

At the owner's volume the module costs about $3/month; Opus 5 with a cached rubric would be about $6. Cost has stopped being an input to the model decision — the eval fixtures decide it (see §6).

Why one call per message: batching ten messages per call would save roughly $1.50/month and cost ALF-158's per-item isolation, where one malformed verdict damages exactly one row. At this volume that is a bad trade. The Batches API's 50% discount is a trap here — it is asynchronous with up to 24-hour turnaround, and a firewall that reports the urgent thing tomorrow is not a firewall.

D16Notification via the existing Telegram bot, ASAP only.

Reuse the channel the notifications spike already settled: a private Telegram bot, one fetch from the Worker. Fires on ASAP and nothing else.

Why: the channel decision is inherited, not relitigated — that spike explicitly named this module as its consumer. ASAP-only because a push for a Today item is the interruption the module exists to remove. A tier that notifies is a tier that costs attention; only one should.

3 · Architecture

Two ingestion homes, one classifier. The daemon carries no model call and no key.

3.1 Data model

New tables, in database/migrations/<next>_comms.sql (the implementation session takes whatever migration number is free — never pin one in a spec).

TableHoldsNotes
comm_accounts One row per mailbox / channel Kind (gmail / imap / imessage), display label, ingestion home. Lets the UI say which account a message arrived at, and lets one account be paused without touching the others.
comm_messages The mirrored message Account, source id (Gmail message id / IMAP UID / chat.db ROWID), sender handle, thread key, body, received-at, plus a deep link back to the source. Uniqueness on (account, source id) so a re-poll is idempotent.
comm_verdicts The model's judgment Tier, whether a reply is owed, the one-line ask, the reason, the rubric version and model that produced it. Separate from the message so a re-classification is a new row, not a destroyed one.
comm_people + comm_handles The people list (D12) A person with a priority; many handles per person. The join is what resolves one human across a phone number and three addresses.
comm_rubrics Versioned rubric text (D11) Append-only. Every verdict names the version that produced it, so "why did it say that" is answerable after an edit.

Triage state lives on the message, not the verdict — the owner marking something handled is a human act, and it must survive a re-classification that changes the tier.

Write-back asymmetry. Gmail can carry alfred's triage state back as a label — Steve's "archive" is a Gmail label change, which is what keeps his two inboxes from diverging. iMessage has no labels and no archive, so its triage state is alfred-local only and will drift from the unread badges in Messages.app. There is no fixing that; it is a property of the platform. Whether Gmail write-back is built at all is an open question (§9).

3.2 What the model is asked for

One call per message, structured output, every field enum'd or short. Following ALF-158's verdict shape:

{
  "tier":       "asap" | "today" | "whenever" | "fyi",
  "owes_reply": true | false,
  "ask":        string,   // the obligation in one line — D14
  "reason":     string    // why this tier, for auditing the rubric
}

The prompt is assembled from three parts, in cache-friendly order (stable first): the task instructions and tier definitions; the rubric document (D11); the rendered people list (D12); then the message itself.

Do not plan around prompt caching on Haiku. Haiku 4.5's minimum cacheable prefix is 4,096 tokens — the highest of any current model, where Opus 5's is 512. A rubric-plus-people prompt of ~1,500 tokens silently will not cache: no error, just cache_creation_input_tokens: 0 forever. The cost figures in §6 assume no caching because none is available. This is worth re-checking if the model ever changes.

5 · Constraints & non-goals

  • No calendar half. Steve's Radar is half filtered-calendar with per-event shortcut buttons. That is a different data source and a different UI, and it is out of this epic.
  • No automatic task or code creation. Spin-off is a deliberate human act (D1).
  • No newsletter reading, summarizing or digesting. Bulk mail is detected and shelved (D13); making it useful is the Reader module's job (SPEC §13.1).
  • No WhatsApp, Slack, Signal, or Twitter. Steve ingests all of them; this epic covers the owner's four actual channels and nothing else.
  • No voicemail integration. iOS Visual Voicemail is programmatically inaccessible. If voicemail is wanted, the path is a carrier or Google Voice setting that emails the transcript — at which point it is simply another email and costs this epic nothing.
  • No dylib injection into Messages.app. The spike notes that read receipts, typing indicators and reactions are reachable that way, at the cost of disabling SIP. Not worth it for a triage tool; read-only plus AppleScript is the ceiling.
  • alfred is never the store of record (D2) — no story may introduce state that exists only in alfred and would be a real loss.
  • Ingestion freshness is bounded by the Mac. While it sleeps, iMessage and WorkMail stop arriving. The module should say so visibly rather than look empty and current.

6 · What it costs to run

Haiku 4.5 at $1.00/MTok input, $5.00/MTok output. Roughly 1,500 tokens of rubric and instructions per call (uncached — see the callout in §3.2), plus the message, plus ~130 tokens of verdict.

Per messageHeavy day (15 texts + 30 emails)Per month
~$0.0022 (iMessage) / ~$0.0026 (email)~$0.11~$3.30 worst case

Sanity check: ALF-158 estimated $1–2/month for ~10 Inbox items a day; the same per-call arithmetic puts that at $0.72, so these figures are in the right order of magnitude. Opus 5 with a cached rubric would run about $5.80/month — a $2.50 difference, which is why D15 makes the model an evaluation question rather than a budget one.

7 · How the work splits into stories

A sketch of the slices and why they cut where they do — not specs. Each is refined in its own session. The ordering matters: A–C are shippable with no model call and no Mac component, so the risky, unfamiliar parts land against something already proven.

#SliceWhy it's its own story
AThe Comms module shell — route group, third module in ModuleRouter / ShellNav, blue accent, empty state isCodePath() is a binary today; a third module turns module resolution into a real router. Entirely deterministic, no data, and it is the change every other slice renders into.
BSchema + the authenticated ingest endpoint — comm_accounts, comm_messages, dedupe on (account, source id) The contract both ingestion homes write to. Proving idempotency here means a re-poll or a double-delivery later is a non-event. No model, no OAuth.
CThe triage UI against seeded data — four tiers, counts, the collapsed FYI shelf, message detail The surface the owner will actually judge the epic by, buildable before a single real message exists. Storybook and E2E cover it deterministically, which they cannot once verdicts are non-deterministic.
DGmail ingestion in the Worker — OAuth for two accounts, polling, the newsletter header filter (D13) First real source, and the one with no new deployment target. Carries the OAuth-refresh-token learning on its own. The header filter belongs here because it is the thing that keeps the volume sane from day one.
EThe Mac daemon skeleton — launchd, health check, ingest client, no sources yet A genuinely new deployment target for this repo: something alfred ships that runs on the owner's machine, with its own install and update story. Worth proving empty before it carries data.
FiMessage in the daemon — chat.db polling, attributedBody decode, contact resolution The spike's whole surface area. Likely wraps imsg rather than re-deriving the decode. Its own story because the failure modes (FDA revocation, schema drift, WAL churn) are unlike anything else in the repo.
GWorkMail over IMAP in the daemon Rides the same daemon but shares nothing with F except the ingest client — a different protocol, different auth, different failure modes.
HThe people list — comm_people / comm_handles, the editor, handle resolution across channels (D12) A migration, a join, and a UI. Blocks J: the classifier can't weigh who a sender is until one human resolves to their phone number and three addresses.
IThe rubric — versioned document, editor, injection into the prompt (D11) The mechanism by which the owner owns the policy. Small, but it is the difference between a system that improves and one that is argued with.
JThe classifier — prompt assembly, enum'd verdict, the floor rule (D5), the write, per-failure degradation The actual model integration, landing behind everything above so its blast radius is one function. Needs H and I for its two prompt inputs.
KEvaluation fixtures + a recall-focused accuracy run (D7) What makes "should this be Opus" a measurement rather than a vibe (D15), and the only place the false-negative rate becomes visible. Needs real messages with expected tiers.
LActions on a row — mark handled, deep-link to the source, and the one-key bridge to an Inbox item Where the module stops being a report. The bridge is the interesting part (see §9) and the readiness rules per source are not uniform.
MTelegram push on ASAP (D16) Small — one fetch from the Worker against a channel the notifications spike already designed. Independent of everything except J producing a tier.

A → B → C ships a real, inspectable module with no LLM and no Mac component in the repo at all. D makes it real for two accounts. E → F → G is the Mac chapter and can be paused without stranding anything. H → I → J is the feature. K, L and M are independent of each other and can be ordered by appetite.

8 · Open questions

  1. Is there a Mac that is realistically awake most of the time? It bounds freshness for two of four sources (D9), and the answer decides whether the module needs a visible staleness indicator or merely a nice-to-have one. Asked, not yet answered — settle before story E.
  2. Does alfred write triage state back to Gmail as a label? Steve's archive is a label change, which is what stops his two inboxes diverging. Read-only is simpler and safer, but leaves Gmail showing everything unread and the owner processing twice. Note the asymmetry either way: iMessage has no equivalent, so its state is alfred-local regardless. Decide in story L.
  3. Does alfred notice when the owner replies in Gmail? If not, a handled message stays in the queue until it is marked handled a second time, by hand — which is exactly the kind of double-bookkeeping that makes a surface get abandoned. Gmail's thread state could answer it cheaply; iMessage's is_from_me could too. Genuinely unresolved, and it may matter more than write-back. Decide in story L.
  4. Is the one-key bridge to an Inbox item built? The owner's decision is that task spin-off stays manual — "I'll do that manually by popping over to the inbox." The concern, which is the owner's own diagnosis turned on the design: the bridge from "this needs a response" to "there is now a task" is a context switch performed at the exact moment of being reminded of an unwanted obligation. That is the step an avoidance pattern skips. Proposed: keep the decision, but make it one keystroke from the Comms row so nothing is actually "popped over" to. Not confirmed.
  5. Do alfred's own Telegram notifications become a fourth source? The notifications spike anticipated exactly this — alfred's messages surfaced in the firewall as their own labeled stream. It is the one inbound integration that is already half-built. Not confirmed, and it is additive rather than blocking.
  6. What are the owner's idiosyncratic action verbs? Steve's are forward-to-wife-and-archive, lightweight reply, and punt-to-Gmail — and he is emphatic that these bespoke shortcuts are what make the tool usable daily rather than a demo. The owner's will be different and are unknown. Story L cannot be specced without them.
  7. How is a stale rubric version handled? Every verdict names the rubric that produced it (D11), which makes a bulk re-classification after a rubric edit possible. Whether it happens automatically, on demand, or never is open. The risk of automatic is the one ALF-158 guards against — the machine changing an answer the human already saw.
