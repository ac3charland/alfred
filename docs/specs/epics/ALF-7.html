ALF-7 — Communication Firewall Module

Epic spec Durable context for the epic. Read this before refining any story inside it.

The one-line shape. A new Comms module aggregates four accounts — two Gmail, one Amazon WorkMail, and iMessage — and shows the owner one question: what do I owe someone a reply to? A small model reads each arriving message against a hand-written rubric and a list of important people, and assigns it to one of four tiers. Only messages that ask something of the owner are queued; everything else falls to an unbadged FYI shelf. alfred never becomes the store of record — every message stays where it already lives, and the module is a view over it.

Revision 2 — what changed. A second round with the owner settled the questions this document opened with, and reversed one decision:

  • D11 was wrong and is rewritten. It ruled out few-shot examples on the strength of an argument that only ruled out replacing the rubric with a learned loop. Prose and examples are complementary; the classifier now carries both.
  • The Mac is not assumed to be always on (D17) — which bounds the module's promise and puts a liveness indicator on the surface.
  • New: reply detection and no Gmail write-back (D18), on-demand re-classification only (D19), and the row's action verbs (D20).
  • Answered and folded in: WorkMail stays on the Mac for now; creating an Inbox item from a message is in scope; alfred's own Telegram channel is a later epic's problem.

Revision 3 — what a review round closed. No settled decision was reversed. What changed is that the document stopped leaving things unsaid:

  • Eight gaps became decisions — D21 (ingestion watermark), D22 (how the daemon authenticates), D23 (the Gmail OAuth client's publishing status), D24 (undecodable input), D25 (per-failure degradation), D26 (attachments and non-text asks), D27 (retention and source deletion), D28 (iMessage edits and unsends). Each closes a hole a story session would otherwise have filled by guessing.
  • Eight existing decisions were widened, not reversed — see the revised pill. The text shown on a revised decision is the current one; nothing below is history.
  • Corrected facts: the Opus comparison in §6 understated the cost by about 3× (it assumed a cache that mostly won't be warm), the message: scheme is not macOS-only, WorkMail has an end-of-support date, and the "no IMAP in Workers" claim was stronger than the evidence.
  • Two things the document was claiming without paying for: §6 now budgets upkeep with a stop condition, and §8 carries three new questions (Q6–Q8) where this spec's own logic points somewhere the owner hasn't decided to go.

1 · Problem space & why now

SPEC.md has named this module since the beginning. §13.2 describes it as "an LLM with access to email / messaging … triages incoming messages by priority and urgency … presented in a dedicated triage UI", §5.1 reserves it a top-level tab, and §5.2 reserves the (firewall) route group. The accent is already assigned: Firewall = blue (--color-accent-blue, #60a5fa).

What the owner is actually buying

This is the part that shapes every decision below, and it is not what the SPEC's one-line description implies.

The prior art for this epic is Steve Newman's "Radar", demonstrated on the Cognitive Revolution podcast (transcript committed at docs/communication-firewall/). Steve's return on it was volume-driven: a few hundred messages a day across five apps, checked around thirty times a day. The firewall bought back hours.

The owner's volume is roughly 15 texts and 30 emails on a heavy day — an order of magnitude less. At that volume the time saved is maybe fifteen minutes, and a rubric hand-tuned exception by exception is a real recurring cost against it — a cost this epic now budgets explicitly, with a stop condition, in §6. Time is not the win. In the owner's words:

What it does buy is clarity and fewer items in my working memory — it lets me abstract "what do I need to respond to" to the single bit of "check comms". I'm afraid of (continuing to) put off or miss important communications because I get overwhelmed by triaging all of it. I'm fixing an avoidance pattern.

Three things follow, and they are the reason this epic looks different from the SPEC's sketch:

  • The product is a response queue, not a unified inbox. A message that asks nothing of the owner has no business in a queue whose whole purpose is answering "do I owe anyone anything."
  • A small, honest count is the mechanism — and zero is its resting state, not its normal one. "Check comms" only collapses to one glance if the number is small and moving: a handful of correctly-triaged obligations that drain as the owner answers them. Two things keep it there, and neither is the classifier being stingy: only obligations enter at all (D3), and replying clears the row without a second act of bookkeeping (D18). Deliberate over-surfacing (D7) pushes the other way, which is why D7 now bounds where that bias is allowed to spend itself. A count that is routinely large, or that only ever grows, is the module failing — not the module working.
  • A false negative is the only failure that matters. One genuinely missed message doesn't cost that message — it costs trust, and the owner resumes checking Gmail "just in case", leaving two inboxes and a worse problem than before.

What this epic builds on

  • ALF-158 (LLM processing) — the Inbox classifier established the pattern this epic reuses: a cron'd Cloudflare Worker sweep, a small model, an enum'd structured verdict, a service-role write, and a per-failure degradation table. The prompt and the correction loop are Inbox-specific and are not reused (see D11).
  • The iMessage ingestion spike — settles that iMessage has no cloud API, that chat.db polling on a Mac is the only viable read path, and how to do it without the reliability gremlins.
  • The outbound-notifications spike — already chose a private Telegram bot, sent from the existing Worker, as alfred's way of reaching the owner. It explicitly anticipated this module as the consumer.

2 · Decisions

What the module is

D1Comms is its own module with its own tables. A message is never an item.

New route group, new tab, new Supabase tables. Messages do not enter the generic-item core, and nothing auto-converts a message into a task or a code item.

Why: the lifecycles don't match. An item is captured, classified, filed and completed; a message arrives, is answered elsewhere, and is never "done" in alfred's sense. SPEC sizes the item core for hundreds of active items — email volume would drown the Inbox and break every existing count and view. The owner also wants spin-off to be a deliberate act: "If tasks/code spring off from communications, I'll do that manually."

D2alfred mirrors. It is never the store of record.

Every message stays in Gmail or Messages.app. alfred holds a copy, a verdict, and triage state. Losing alfred's database loses the interface and nothing else.

Why: this is the load-bearing property of Steve's system and the reason he can ship to production with no staging — "all my messages really live where they've always been… if this totally falls down, everything is still where it's always been. I just lose the nice interface to it." It also means retention and deletion in alfred are safe by construction.

D3Only messages that ask something of the owner enter the queue.

Obligation is the entry ticket, not a sort key. A message that needs no reply is never queued — it lands on the FYI shelf (D6).

Why: the premise is avoidance, not time. The bit the owner wants is "do I owe anyone anything", and a queue containing no-action mail cannot answer it. Non-actionable mail is already handled fine in Gmail and Messages directly.

D4Four tiers: ASAP / Today / Whenever / FYI.

TierMeaningBadge
ASAPBreak focus for it. Someone is blocked on the owner, it is time-critical, or the sender is important enough that delay costs something real.Counted
TodayNeeds a reply before the owner logs off; not worth interrupting for. Most real correspondence.Counted
WheneverA reply is genuinely owed, but nothing turns on the date.Counted
FYIEverything else. No reply owed.None — collapsed by default

Why the shape diverges from Steve's: for Steve, the bottom tier is largely the junk pile. Here junk never enters the queue at all, so Whenever means owed but undated — which is precisely the three-week-avoidance bucket. It has to stay visible rather than being where things go to die.

D5One blended priority, with obligation as a floor.

The model returns a single tier, not a grid. Whether the owner owes a reply is an input to that one number — but an obligation never falls below Whenever.

Why: the owner asked for one number to glance at, and a 2×2 urgency × obligation grid gives four. But a pure urgency sort buries the non-urgent-but-owed message at the bottom — reintroducing the exact failure the epic exists to fix. The floor rule keeps one number while making that class of message structurally un-buriable.

D6Everything is stored, and FYI is the shelf it lands onrevised

Every ingested message is persisted and searchable regardless of tier. FYI is an unbadged, collapsed shelf the owner can open to spot-check the rubric.

Why: once only obligations are queued (D3), a misjudged message is invisible rather than merely buried — a strictly worse failure mode. Storing everything is what keeps the evidence of a false negative in the building, and FYI is the surface on which the rubric's recall can be audited at all. Unbadged so it reads as an archive, not a to-do.

What this decision does not buy, stated plainly: it makes a false negative recoverable, not recovered. Those are different claims and only the first one follows. The shelf is unbadged, uncounted and always large by design, and the owner it is built for was selected by an avoidance pattern — so "open FYI and check" asks for exactly the voluntary triage the module exists to remove. Nothing in this epic forces that audit to happen. The one place recall is measured on purpose is story K's fixtures (D7), which sample rather than watch; whether the shelf also needs a forcing function is §8 Q8, deliberately left open rather than answered with an unbadged badge.

D7Recall over precision — at the queue boundary, not at the ASAP boundaryrevised

The rubric is tuned to over-surface, and false negatives are the metric that decides in the evaluation fixtures. But the bias has a direction as well as a size: uncertainty resolves into the queue and down to Today. A message the model is unsure about is queued rather than shelved — and lands on Today, never on ASAP.

Why the asymmetry: one missed obligation costs the module its entire reason to exist. A message wrongly queued as Today costs a glance, which is genuinely a rounding error at ~45 messages a day.

Why the direction, and the tension it resolves: a spurious ASAP does not cost seconds — D16 makes ASAP the one tier that pushes to the owner's phone, so an unwarranted ASAP is an unwarranted interruption, the exact cost this whole module exists to remove. An unbounded recall bias would buy recall with several of those a day, on the one tier D16 keeps sacred. So recall is bought at the two boundaries where it is cheap (is this an obligation at all; is it Today or Whenever) and not at the one where it is expensive. ASAP is tuned for precision on purpose, and story K's fixtures score the two boundaries separately: recall on "did it reach the queue", precision on "did it push".

What this costs, said out loud: the module's own premise is a small count (§1), and a recall bias is a machine for making counts bigger. The bound above is what keeps those two claims compatible — over-surfacing shows up as one more row on Today, which drains on reply (D18), rather than as a buzzing phone. If the Today count is routinely large enough to be avoided rather than read, the bias is mistuned and story K is where that becomes visible.

Ingestion

D8The Mac daemon is a dumb pipe. All triage runs in the Worker.

The daemon polls, normalizes, and POSTs the full message to an authenticated alfred ingest endpoint (D22 settles what "authenticated" means). It contains no model call, holds no Anthropic key, and holds no Supabase credential.

Why: one classifier and one rubric across all four accounts. The alternative — triaging locally and shipping only a verdict — means two rubrics that drift apart, a second Anthropic key on a laptop when workers/src/index.ts (lines 32–33) says the Worker's is "the only place in the whole system that holds one", and a Comms view that can't show the message it's asking about. Accepted cost: personal iMessage bodies, including other people's words, are copied into Supabase and cross the internet to get there. Single-user project, TLS in transit, RLS on, the owner's own instance — weighed and accepted. D22 is what makes "authenticated" more than a word, and D27 says how long the copy lives.

D9Two ingestion homes: the Worker for Gmail, the Mac for iMessage and WorkMailrevised

SourceHomeMechanism
Gmail ×2 (personal + one RealPlay)Cloudflare WorkerGmail API, one OAuth refresh token each, polled on the existing cron
Amazon WorkMailMac daemonIMAP
iMessage / SMSMac daemon~/Library/Messages/chat.db, read-only

Why: iMessage has no cloud API of any kind, so the daemon must exist regardless — that is the load-bearing half of this decision. WorkMail is neither Gmail nor Microsoft Graph; it is Amazon's Exchange-compatible service and its practical read path is IMAP. Since the daemon exists anyway, WorkMail rides along at near-zero marginal cost. Accepted cost: the Mac is a single point of failure for two of four sources.

On IMAP in the Workers runtime — the honest version. An earlier revision said no IMAP client is known to run there. That overstated it: Workers can open raw TCP via cloudflare:sockets, cf-imap is written for exactly that runtime, and ImapFlow's ESM build is reported to work under nodejs_compat. What is true is narrower — nothing in this repo has tried any of them, and most Node IMAP libraries still assume net/tls. So "IMAP can't run in the Worker" is not why WorkMail sits on the Mac; it sits there because the daemon is already being built and shipping WorkMail through it costs one client rather than one deployment target. If the Mac's intermittency (D17) ever bites hard enough, moving WorkMail into the Worker is a live option and not a rewrite.

A dated constraint on this channel, because it changes what story G is worth. AWS has published an end of support for Amazon WorkMail: no new customers after 2026-04-30, and the service ends entirely on 2027-03-31. As of this document (2026-09-09) that is under seven months of remaining life — plausibly less than the time between this epic starting and story G being built. The owner's choice to keep WorkMail on the Mac "for now" is therefore load-bearing rather than incidental: the channel itself is temporary, and whatever the mailbox migrates to will almost certainly speak IMAP too, so the daemon's IMAP client outlives WorkMail even though the account does not. Whether the channel warrants story G at all is §8 Q6.

D10The daemon polls. It does not watch the filesystem.

Poll on last-seen ROWID, every few seconds, with a read-only SQLite connection. Wrapped in a launchd agent with a health check that fails loudly.

Why: straight from the spike. macOS coalesces FSEvents for background processes it considers idle, so a pure watcher silently stalls for minutes on a quiet machine — exactly the condition under which a firewall must keep working. Read-only avoids contention with Messages.app, which holds the write lock. The loud health check exists because Full Disk Access grants have been observed to reset silently across macOS updates, and a firewall that fails quietly is worse than one that fails.

D11The prompt carries a hand-edited rubric and a curated example set. Both are versionedrevised

Two artifacts, both editable in alfred, both stamped on every verdict:

  • A rubric — prose policy the owner can state outright. "Anything from my wife is ASAP."
  • An example set — corrected messages with their right answer, fed as few-shot examples. Every correction made through D20's tier dropdown becomes an example automatically; the owner can prune one that turns out to steer badly.

Why both: prose states the policy you can articulate; examples teach the judgment you can't. "This colleague writes in a way that sounds urgent but never is" is not a rule that survives being written down — it is exactly what an example conveys. Versioning both and stamping the verdict with both is what keeps "why did it say that" answerable, which is the property that actually matters. (Un-auditability comes from not knowing which examples were in a given prompt — not from having examples.)

What this does not adopt from ALF-158: corrections do not replace policy. The rubric stays the primary, human-authored statement of what matters; examples refine its edges. ALF-158's hardest open question — which corrections earn their tokens — barely applies here: at ~45 messages a day and a $3 bill, every correction can be carried for a long time before prompt budget is worth a thought.

Revised from revision 1, which rejected few-shot examples outright. That decision conflated replacing the rubric with augmenting it, and was wrong.

D12The people list is structured data, keyed on a personrevised

A contacts table — a person, their priority, and their many handles (a phone number, three email addresses). Rendered into the prompt; edited in the UI, not in prose.

Why: "these people are important" is a lookup, not a judgment. Keeping it out of the prose rubric makes it editable in one place and auditable ("flagged because it's from Dana"). Keying on the person rather than the address is forced by the multi-channel design: the same human is a phone number in iMessage and an email address in two mailboxes.

What structure does not buy — a claim an earlier revision overstated. A roster is not "immune to the drift a paragraph suffers"; it drifts differently, and arguably worse. Stale prose is at least visible when the owner next reads the rubric. A stale roster fails silently: a new colleague, a changed number, a second address get no floor-rule protection at all, and nothing announces the gap — the message simply lands a tier low, or on the FYI shelf, and looks like an ordinary verdict. That is a false negative arriving through the one input the owner believed was exact.

The mitigation, and its limit. The roster must be editable from the place the gap shows up — the message that was mistiered — rather than only from a settings page, so "promote this sender" is one act instead of a context switch. Where exactly that affordance sits (a fifth row verb, the message detail, the tier dropdown's neighbour) is story H's to place, and the §4 mockup deliberately doesn't draw it, so no story should inherit a shape this document never signed off. Even with it, the trigger is the owner noticing — which is the same unforced audit D6 declines to claim credit for.

D13Newsletters never reach the classifier.

Detected deterministically from headers — List-Unsubscribe, List-ID, Precedence: bulk — before any model call, and filed straight to FYI. They are reserved for the future Reader module (SPEC §13.1).

Why: bulk mail is always non-urgent and never an obligation, so a model call to decide that is pure waste. Steve runs Radar and his RSS reader as separate tools for the same reason. Headers answer it for free, and keeping the whale out of the prompt keeps both the counts and the bill honest.

The caveat, because the coverage claim is weaker than it sounds. List-Unsubscribe, List-ID and Precedence: bulk are conventions, not guarantees. Big ESPs set them; smaller senders, one-off mail-merge blasts and plenty of transactional mail do not. So the filter will leak in both directions, and the two leaks are not equally cheap:

  • A newsletter that slips through costs one Haiku call (~$0.0026) and lands on FYI with a verdict and a reason attached. Tolerable, and self-correcting — the example set learns the shape (D11).
  • A real message wrongly filtered is the expensive one. It never reaches the classifier, so it has no verdict and no reason — it sits on the shelf indistinguishable from mail the model judged, which makes it strictly harder to audit than a mistiered message. That is the D6 failure mode arriving through a deterministic path.

What follows for the story: filter on strong signals only (a genuine list header, not a lone Precedence), and record on the message that the filter, not the model, put it there (§3.1) — so "never asked" is distinguishable from "asked and answered no" when the shelf is reviewed. A message with a known sender in comm_people (D12) is never filtered, whatever its headers say.

D21Every source starts from a bounded lookback window, never from zero and never from "now".

Each account carries its own cursor on comm_accounts — a chat.db ROWID, an IMAP UIDVALIDITY+UID, a Gmail historyId. On an account's first run the cursor is seeded from a 7-day lookback, and everything older than the window is never ingested at all.

Why the window has to be decided here and not in each story: both defaults a story would reach for are wrong, in opposite directions. Starting at ROWID 0 classifies years of chat.db history — tens of thousands of messages, a Haiku bill in the tens or hundreds of dollars against a $3.30 budget (§6), a queue full of obligations that died in 2023, and (without care) a Telegram push for every ancient ASAP. Starting at max silently drops everything that arrived before the daemon's first tick, which is a false negative on day one, delivered by the system that exists to prevent them. A week is long enough that nothing live is missed at handover and short enough that the backfill is a rounding error on the bill.

Three consequences worth writing down: messages ingested by the initial backfill are classified but never pushed — a six-day-old ASAP must not buzz the phone (D16). A re-seed is safe, because uniqueness on (account, source id) makes re-ingestion a no-op (story B). And the cursor is a resumption point, not a record of intent: an IMAP UIDVALIDITY change or an expired Gmail historyId (Gmail's history is only good for about a week) re-seeds from the window rather than starting over. Historical backfill beyond the window is out of scope — if it is ever wanted it is a deliberate, separate act, and D19 already says nothing gets silently re-judged.

D22The daemon authenticates with an HMAC signature over the raw body — the pattern the repo already runs.

The ingest endpoint verifies a sha256=… signature exactly the way the existing GitHub webhook does: verifySignature in workers/src/hmac.ts, called from workers/src/index.ts the way GITHUB_WEBHOOK_SECRET is today. Comms gets its own secret — set with wrangler secret put, never shared with the webhook.

Why not something else: D8 accepts that personal message bodies — including other people's words — cross the internet, and that acceptance is only defensible if the endpoint that receives them cannot be posted to by anyone who guesses the URL. The repo already has the right primitive, tested and in production; inventing a bearer token or an API key here would be a second auth scheme for no gain. Sign the raw body (not a parsed re-serialization) and include a timestamp inside the signed payload, so a captured request can't be replayed to resurrect old messages later — dedupe (D21) already makes replay mostly harmless, but "mostly" is not a security property.

Where the credential lives on the Mac: the login keychain, read by the daemon at start (security find-generic-password). Not in the launchd plist — those are world-readable — not in a dotfile beside the code, and never in this repo. The daemon holds this one secret and nothing else: no Supabase key, no Anthropic key (D8), so the blast radius of a stolen laptop is "can write messages into alfred", not "can read alfred".

D23The Gmail OAuth client ships in "In production", not "Testing".

Before story D ships, the Google Cloud OAuth client's publishing status must be In production. Its refresh tokens are then stored as Worker secrets, one per account.

Why this is a named constraint and not a footnote: a client left in Testing issues refresh tokens that expire after 7 days. Nothing warns you; the tokens simply stop working. That would kill both Gmail accounts a week after story D ships — the two sources that carry the entire ASAP latency promise (D17), since the other two ride a Mac that sleeps. The one reliability guarantee this module makes would fail quietly, seven days in, for a reason that has nothing to do with the code.

The rest of the lifecycle, so it isn't rediscovered: gmail.readonly is a restricted scope, so an unverified production client shows the owner an "unverified app" screen on first consent — a warning to click through, not a blocker, for a personal client whose only user is the owner. Even a production refresh token can still die: a Google password change, an explicit revoke, six months of non-use, or too many outstanding tokens for one client. So the failure is rarer, not impossible — which is exactly why D17's health surface has to cover Gmail and not just the Mac.

D24A message alfred can't decode is stored and flagged. It is never skipped.

A failed attributedBody decode, an odd MIME part, an encoding the parser doesn't know: the row is still written, with the body it managed to extract (possibly empty) and a flag saying extraction failed. The poll loop logs it and moves on — one bad row never stalls ingestion.

Why "skip it" is not available: a skipped message is a false negative by construction, and the worst kind — it leaves no trace anywhere in alfred, so not even the FYI shelf (D6) can be audited into finding it. The owner would have no way to learn it existed. Storing a row with an empty body costs nothing and keeps the failure visible, which is the property the whole epic is organised around.

What happens to it then: it is handed to the classifier like anything else, but a message flagged as unreadable is never allowed to be judged FYI on the strength of empty text — it takes the D26 path and lands in the queue, marked, where Open in source (D20) is the recovery: the message still lives in Messages.app or the mailbox, which is exactly what D2 guarantees.

The verdict

D14The verdict states the ask, not the topic.

The per-message summary answers "what does this want from me, and by when" — not "what is this about". "Dana needs the invoice approved before Friday", never "regarding the Q3 invoice".

Why: what makes triage aversive is ambiguity about whether a message contains an obligation. A topic summary preserves that ambiguity and forces the owner to open the message to resolve it — which is the exact work the module is supposed to remove.

D15Haiku 4.5 to start, chosen by evaluation rather than by price. One call per message.

At the owner's volume the module costs about $3.30/month on Haiku 4.5. Opus 5 would run roughly $17–19/month at the same volume — see §6, which names the caching assumption rather than quietly assuming a warm cache. That is a real 5× ratio on a small absolute number, so the eval fixtures still decide the model (story K) — but if Opus ever wins on eval, the cost is worth re-reading rather than waved through as noise.

Why one call per message: batching ten messages per call would save roughly $1.50/month and cost ALF-158's per-item isolation, where one malformed verdict damages exactly one row. At this volume that is a bad trade. The Batches API's 50% discount is a trap here — it is asynchronous with up to 24-hour turnaround, and a firewall that reports the urgent thing tomorrow is not a firewall.

D16Notification via the existing Telegram bot, ASAP only.

Reuse the channel the notifications spike already settled: a private Telegram bot, one fetch from the Worker. Fires on ASAP and nothing else.

Why: the channel decision is inherited, not relitigated — that spike explicitly named this module as its consumer. ASAP-only because a push for a Today item is the interruption the module exists to remove. A tier that notifies is a tier that costs attention; only one should.

D25Per-failure degradation — and a message without a verdict is never invisible.

One stateless request per message, no conversational repair loop (following ALF-158's D13). What happens depends on what failed:

What went wrongWhat the sweep does
429 / 5xx / timeout / network Nothing written. The message stays unclassified and the next sweep picks it up — the sweep is the retry, exactly as in ALF-158. No queue, no backoff state.
Malformed output — unparseable, or a tier outside the enum The verdict is discarded and the attempt counted. No re-ask: the same prompt will mostly produce the same shape.
stop_reason: "max_tokens" Discarded, attempt counted. A config bug rather than model behaviour — the verdict is ~130 tokens, so this should never fire.
stop_reason: "refusal" No retry — re-sending won't help. Straight to the unjudged path below. Worth expecting here in a way it isn't for Inbox items: these are other people's words, not the owner's.
No API key, or the API is down for a whole sweep Nothing written, nothing marked. Ingestion keeps running — messages still arrive and are still stored (D6). This is a classifier outage, not a source outage, and D17's health surface has to say so in those words.
Attempt ceiling reached — the message has no verdict and won't get one It enters the queue as Today, flagged unjudged, with an ask that says so. Counted. Never pushed (D16) — a tier alfred didn't choose may not buzz the phone. It stays re-runnable on demand (D19).

Why the last row is the whole decision: every other outcome is a retry. The only question that matters is what happens to a message the model will never successfully judge, and there are exactly two answers. Leave it out of the queue and it is invisible — the precise failure D6 and D7 exist to prevent, arriving through the machinery meant to prevent it, and worse than a mistiered message, because there is no verdict to audit and no reason to read. Or put it in the queue, marked, and pay one glance. That is not a close call.

The tension with D3, stated rather than buried: D3 says only obligations enter the queue, and an unjudged message has not been shown to be one. It is admitted anyway because unknown is not nothing — D7's direction is that uncertainty resolves into the queue and down to Today, and total uncertainty is still uncertainty. This is the one class of row in the queue that hasn't earned its place, it is marked as such on its face, and if it is ever more than a rarity that is a bug in the classifier, not a tier to get used to.

D26A message whose ask isn't in its text is flagged, not judged on the empty text.

comm_messages carries a small content signal — whether the message has attachments or images, and whether its body extracted cleanly (D24). A message with little or no readable text, whether because its content is in an attachment or because extraction failed, is never filed FYI on the strength of that text: it is queued at Today, flagged, and the row says why.

Why: "sign this" with a photo of a form is an ordinary, real obligation whose entire content is in the image. Handed the text alone, the classifier isn't uncertain — it confidently and correctly reports that nothing was asked, and the message disappears onto an unbadged shelf. That is a structural false negative: not a tuning miss the example set can learn from (D11), but a class of message the pipeline cannot see, failing the same way every time. Flagging costs one row on Today and converts a silent miss into a visible one.

Scope, deliberately small: the MVP does not send images to the model. D15 is one text call per message and that stands. The flag is the honest minimum — it says "alfred can't read this one, you look" — and it is also what makes the upgrade targetable: if attachment-carrying messages turn out to be common, a later story can route exactly those rows to a vision-capable call without re-classifying anything else (D19).

Living with the constraints

D17No source is assumed to be working. The module says so on its face, per accountrevised

Every account carries its own health: last_seen_at, stamped on every successful poll — not on every message — plus the last error and when it happened. The Comms header shows one indicator per account, in three states:

StateMeansTypical cause
LivePolled successfully within this source's expected intervalNormal
StaleNo successful poll for longer than that interval — nothing is arriving and alfred doesn't know what it's missingThe Mac is asleep or shut (D9); the cron didn't fire
ErroringPolls are running and failing — the account is dead, not quietA rejected Gmail refresh token (D23), a rate limit, IMAP auth refused, Full Disk Access revoked (D10)

Thresholds are per source, because "normal" differs: the Gmail accounts ride the Worker's cron and should be minutes fresh; the Mac's two are expected to go dark overnight. A classifier outage (D25) is a fourth, module-level state — ingestion healthy, judgment stalled — and must not be drawn as a source failure, because the fix is different.

Why per-account, and why "erroring" is its own state: the earlier version of this decision only contemplated the Mac sleeping, which left the exact failure it exists to prevent wide open. A revoked Gmail token or a rate-limited account would leave that dot green while nothing at all was arriving — a surface that looks empty, current and trustworthy over two dead sources. Worse, "quiet" and "broken" are indistinguishable from message volume alone: a mailbox with nothing new looks identical to one that can't be read. Only a successful poll distinguishes them, which is why the heartbeat is stamped on the poll and not on the mail. A firewall's whole value is that its zero means something (§1); a zero that can silently mean "we stopped looking" is worth less than no firewall at all.

The consequence, stated plainly because it is easy to discover late: an ASAP iMessage that arrives at 11pm reaches the owner when the Mac next wakes. The ASAP latency promise holds for the two Gmail accounts only. WorkMail rides the daemon and inherits the same limit — kept there for now (D9) rather than forwarded into Gmail, which remains the escape hatch if the intermittency bites.

D18The queue drains by replying, not by marking things done. alfred never writes to Gmailrevised

Read-only, both directions of the decision: no labels, no archive, no state pushed back to any source. Instead alfred watches — Gmail thread state, and is_from_me on a later message in an iMessage conversation. When the owner replies in their own client, the item clears itself.

Why this beats write-back, which was the obvious design: Steve writes a Gmail label so his two inboxes don't diverge. Detection achieves the same convergence without alfred ever touching the mailbox — and it removes the second bookkeeping step entirely. That step is the one an avoidance pattern skips, so a design where the owner must mark things handled in alfred would fail in the specific way this epic exists to prevent.

There are exactly three ways a row leaves the queue, and every story must honour all three:

  1. The owner replies in their own client → detection clears it. The main path, and the only one that requires nothing of the owner inside alfred.
  2. Not replying (D20) — a conscious decision to leave something unanswered. Reply detection cannot clear a reply that never comes. The fallback, not the main path.
  3. Make an Inbox item (D20) — the obligation moves into the Inbox, which becomes its durable tracker, and the comms row clears at that moment.

Why the third one is not optional: without it, spinning a message into a task is a way out of the message and not out of the queue — the owner handles the obligation in the Inbox, never replies, and the comms row sits on Today forever. That is two trackers for one obligation, which is precisely the duplicated-inbox failure §1 and D1 are organised against, and it would show up as the one symptom this module cannot afford: a count that never reaches zero for reasons the owner can't act on.

D19Re-classification is on demand only. Nothing is silently re-judged.

Editing the rubric or the example set does not sweep the existing queue. A re-run is an explicit action.

Why: this is ALF-158's "the machine changed my answer back" failure. A verdict the owner has already seen — and possibly already acted on — moving underneath them costs more trust than a stale tier costs attention.

D20Four verbs on a row, and a tier dropdown that feeds the example setrevised

VerbDoes
Open in sourceDeep-links to the message in its own client. Desktop only for the MVP — see §3.3.
Make an Inbox itemCreates an alfred item from the message, in one action, without leaving Comms — and clears the comms row (see below).
Not replyingClears something the owner has decided to leave unanswered (D18).

On "Make an Inbox item": the owner's decision is that task spin-off stays a human act, and it does — but the bridge is where an avoidance pattern reasserts itself, since it is a context switch performed at the exact moment of being reminded of an unwanted obligation. One action from the row keeps the decision human and removes the friction.

And it clears the row — which is a decision, not an implementation detail. Making an Inbox item is the third exit from the queue (D18): the Inbox item becomes the obligation's durable tracker, so the comms row has nothing left to track and goes. Without that, the owner spins a message into a task, handles it in the Inbox, never replies — and reply detection never fires, leaving the row on Today forever. Two trackers, one obligation, and a count that won't drain. The message itself is untouched and still in Gmail or Messages (D2); it is the queue row that clears, and the new item carries the link back to it.

What this decision does not settle — keybindings. The verbs are fixed here; their keyboard shortcuts are not, and the §4 mockup deliberately draws none. No row-level hotkey convention exists anywhere in the app today, so inventing four letters in an epic spec would hand every later module a convention nobody designed. Story L proposes the bindings, against the app as a whole rather than against this one row.

On the tier dropdown: because tier membership is also the entry ticket (D3), promoting something out of FYI corrects the obligation judgment rather than the urgency — which makes it the false-negative recovery path D7 cares about, and the most valuable example the set can hold.

Deliberately absent — quick reply and forward. Steve has both (forward-to-wife-and-archive is one of his keys). Each needs send capability — SMTP, or AppleScript into Messages.app — which nothing else in this epic requires. A meaningful scope expansion for a convenience the owner's existing clients already cover.

D27Nothing expires, and a deletion at the source does not propagate. Purging is manual.

alfred's mirror keeps every message indefinitely (D6). A message deleted in Gmail, unsent in Messages, or removed from a mailbox stays in alfred — nothing watches for deletions and nothing reconciles them. In the other direction, deleting something in alfred never touches the source (D18). A manual purge — one message, one account, or everything older than a chosen date — ships with the schema in story B.

Why this is written down rather than left silent: "no retention policy" is a policy, and this module stores other people's private words indefinitely, in a database built to make them readable. D2 is the usual answer here and it only goes one way: it says alfred's copy is safe to lose, not that it is safe to keep. Volume never forces the question — ~45 messages a day is roughly 16,000 rows and a few tens of megabytes a year — so the trigger for revisiting is comfort, not cost: if the owner would hesitate to hand someone this database, the retention window stops being "forever". Deciding that later is cheap; discovering there was never a decision is not.

One interaction worth stating: purging a message removes its body, but a correction that cited it keeps its own denormalised copy of the text — the same shape ALF-158's classification_corrections uses — so the example set (D11) survives a purge instead of quietly losing examples and changing the classifier's behaviour as a side effect of housekeeping.

D28iMessage edits and unsends are invisible to alfred. Accepted, and stated.

The daemon polls forward on ROWID (D10, D21), which sees inserts. An edit rewrites an existing row in place and an unsend blanks or removes one — neither allocates a new ROWID, so a cursor-based poll never revisits either. Two consequences, both accepted:

  • An edited message is triaged on its original wording, forever. The tier, the ask and the reason all describe text that no longer exists in Messages.app.
  • An unsent message persists in alfred's mirror (D27) — and may still be sitting in the queue asking for a reply to something the sender has retracted.

Why accept rather than fix: catching either needs a full-table diff on every poll — which throws away the cursor that makes polling cheap and safe — or dylib injection into Messages.app, an explicit non-goal (§5) that costs SIP. Neither is worth it for a triage tool. And D19 means the fix wouldn't be automatic anyway: even if alfred saw the edit, it would not silently re-judge a verdict the owner may already have acted on.

Why it is survivable: D2. The message lives in Messages.app, and Open in source (D20) is the arbiter — if a row's ask reads oddly, the source is one click away and is always the truth. The failure here is a stale row, which is visible and correctable; it is not a missing one.

3 · Architecture

Two ingestion homes, one classifier. The daemon carries no model call and no key.

Triage state lives on the message, not the verdict — the owner marking something handled is a human act, and it must survive a re-classification that changes the tier.

Access control — the same rules as every other table here

All seven tables get enable row level security, the authenticated full access policy, and explicit grant select, insert, update, delete to anon, authenticated, service_role — plus a grant on any sequence, if a table uses one. This is not boilerplate to skim: raw psql -f migrations don't get Supabase's auto-grants, a missing grant surfaces as a runtime "permission denied" rather than a migration failure, and migration-lint fails check:fast at commit time precisely to stop that reaching production. Single-user project, so role-based RLS and no user_id column (SPEC §7), exactly as in ALF-146 and ALF-158.

Which role writes what — this epic adds a third write path, and it is worth naming because the daemon is the first thing outside Cloudflare and the browser to put rows in this database:

Write-back asymmetry — settled context, not an open question. Steve's system writes a Gmail label, and that label is what keeps his two inboxes from diverging. alfred declines that move by choice: D18 settles that alfred never writes to any source, in either direction. So the asymmetry to know about is what replaces the label. On Gmail, thread state gives reply detection something solid to read. iMessage has no labels and no archive, so its triage state is alfred-local only and will drift from the unread badges in Messages.app — that part is a property of the platform, and there is no fixing it. Neither one is a gap waiting to be filled; the queue drains by replying (D18), and the drift is the accepted cost.

3.2 What the model is asked for

One call per message, structured output, every field enum'd or short. Following ALF-158's verdict shape:

{
  "tier":       "asap" | "today" | "whenever" | "fyi",
  "owes_reply": true | false,
  "ask":        string,   // the obligation in one line — D14
  "reason":     string    // why this tier, for auditing the rubric
}

The prompt is assembled in cache-friendly order (stable first): the task instructions and tier definitions; the rubric document; the example set drawn from comm_corrections (D11); the rendered people list (D12); then the message itself. Each verdict is stamped with the rubric version and the example-set version, so any judgment can be reconstructed.

3.3 Deep links back to the source

Everything is built from the RFC822 Message-ID, captured at ingestion — one stored field, which the two mail clients then want in two different shapes:

TargetLinkWorks
Apple Mail (macOS)message:%3C<Message-ID>%3EDesktop. Undocumented scheme — the story must verify it, not trust this spec.
Apple Mail (iOS)message:%3C<Message-ID>%3E — the same schemeAlso works on iOS Mail, with one real constraint: the message must already be downloaded on that device. Mail resolves the id against its local store, so an id it hasn't synced simply doesn't open. Not "no scheme exists".
Gmail web…/#search/rfc822msgid:<Message-ID>Desktop and mobile. Angle brackets stripped — see the gotcha below.
iMessageimessage:// to the handleOpens the conversation, not the message — the ceiling on that platform
The two mail links want the Message-ID in different shapes. RFC 5322 stores it with angle brackets — <CAF…@mail.gmail.com> — and that is what ingestion captures. Apple Mail's message: scheme wants them kept (percent-encoded as %3C / %3E). Gmail's rfc822msgid: operator wants them stripped: pass the brackets through and the search silently returns nothing — no error, just an empty result page, which reads exactly like "the message isn't there". Normalise at link-build time, not at ingestion: the stored value stays the true RFC 5322 header.

The MVP ships desktop links only — and that is a scoping choice, not a platform limit. The schemes exist on both platforms; alfred is simply used from a desktop and building, testing and verifying two link surfaces is work story L doesn't need to do twice. It scopes the surface, not the data: the Message-ID is captured for every email from story B onward, so adding the mobile surface later is a UI change rather than a backfill.

Do not plan around prompt caching on Haiku. Haiku 4.5's minimum cacheable prefix is 4,096 tokens — the highest of any current model, where Opus 5's is 512. A rubric-plus-people prompt of ~1,500 tokens silently will not cache: no error, just cache_creation_input_tokens: 0 forever. The cost figures in §6 assume no caching because none is available. This is worth re-checking if the model ever changes.

And where caching is available, the traffic defeats it. On a model with a 512-token minimum, the cache entry still lives about five minutes. At ~45 messages a day the mean gap between messages is roughly 32 minutes, and they arrive in a working-hours clump rather than evenly — so most calls find a cold cache and pay the 1.25× write premium instead of the 0.1× read. Any comparison that quietly assumes a warm cache is off by several times, which is why §6 quotes a range and names the assumption.

5 · Constraints & non-goals

  • No calendar half. Steve's Radar is half filtered-calendar with per-event shortcut buttons. That is a different data source and a different UI, and it is out of this epic.
  • No automatic task or code creation. Spin-off is a deliberate human act — but a one-keystroke one (D20).
  • No snooze. Every tool of this shape has one, and it is rejected here on purpose: a snooze button is a machine for deferring things while feeling productive, in a module built to fix an avoidance pattern. If Today becomes a tier the owner ignores wholesale, revisit — that would be evidence the pressure valve was load-bearing after all. (§8 Q7 records the asymmetry this creates with Whenever, which is kept and is also inert.)
  • No sending. No quick reply, no forward (D20) — both need SMTP or AppleScript into Messages.app, which nothing else here requires.
  • No write-back to any source (D18). alfred reads; it never labels, archives or modifies a mailbox.
  • alfred's own Telegram notifications are not a source in this epic. The notifications spike anticipated surfacing them in the firewall as their own labeled stream, and that remains the intent — as a later story, once the four real channels work.
  • No newsletter reading, summarizing or digesting. Bulk mail is detected and shelved (D13); making it useful is the Reader module's job (SPEC §13.1).
  • No WhatsApp, Slack, Signal, or Twitter. Steve ingests all of them; this epic covers the owner's four actual channels and nothing else.
  • No voicemail integration. iOS Visual Voicemail is programmatically inaccessible. If voicemail is wanted, the path is a carrier or Google Voice setting that emails the transcript — at which point it is simply another email and costs this epic nothing.
  • No dylib injection into Messages.app. The spike notes that read receipts, typing indicators and reactions are reachable that way, at the cost of disabling SIP. Not worth it for a triage tool; read-only plus AppleScript is the ceiling.
  • No reading of images or attachments. One text call per message (D15); a message whose ask is inside a photo is flagged, not interpreted (D26). Routing those rows to a vision call is a later story the flag exists to make possible.
  • No retention policy and no deletion sync (D27). Nothing expires on a timer, a deletion at the source does not propagate into alfred's mirror, and purging is a manual act. Silence here would have been a decision too — this is the decision, with a stated trigger for revisiting it.
  • No historical backfill. Each source starts from a 7-day window (D21). Ingesting the archive is a separate, deliberate act, not something a first run does by accident.
  • alfred is never the store of record (D2) — no story may introduce state that exists only in alfred and would be a real loss.
  • Ingestion freshness is bounded by the Mac. While it sleeps, iMessage and WorkMail stop arriving. The module should say so visibly rather than look empty and current.

Cross-check against ALF-158. Its cost table is headed "Per month @ 20/day" and puts its Inbox classifier at ~$1.20 on the same model. The 20% gap is the difference in prompt size — this prompt carries a people list and an example set that ALF-158's doesn't — so the two arithmetics agree, which is all a cross-check is for. What Opus 5 would actually cost — with the assumption named

Opus 5 is $5/MTok in, $25/MTok out: five times Haiku on both sides. Caching is available there (512-token minimum, against Haiku's 4,096 — §3.3), which is the only reason the comparison is interesting at all. But a cache entry lives about five minutes, and at 45 messages a day the mean gap between them is ~32 minutes. So most calls would miss, and a miss with caching enabled costs the 1.25× write premium rather than the 0.1× read:

It is still a small absolute sum for a single-user tool, so D15 stands and story K's fixtures decide the model. But the ratio is 5×, not the rounding error it was described as, and a decision to move to Opus should be taken knowing that.

The other cost: upkeep, and when to stop paying it

§1 names the real risk of this epic — at fifteen minutes of triage saved a day, a rubric hand-tuned exception by exception can cost more than it returns. And the module ships three separately-tunable artifacts: the rubric (D11), the auto-growing example set (D11), and the people roster (D12). That risk was named and then never budgeted. Here is the budget:

The stop condition, stated so it can actually fire: if six weeks after story J the correction rate is not falling, or the owner is editing the rubric more than weekly to chase the same class of mistake, then the tuning loop is not converging and more rubric is not the answer. The moves at that point are a better model (story K makes that a measurement, not a vibe), fewer sources, or a coarser split — and if none of them work, the honest reading is that this module costs more than the fifteen minutes it saves, which §1 already admits is the thing to watch. No instrumentation is needed for any of this: the correction rate is the accuracy metric (ALF-158's insight), and it is recorded automatically by work the owner was doing anyway.

KEvaluation fixtures + an accuracy run scored at both of D7's boundaries — recall on "did it reach the queue", precision on "did it push" What makes "should this be Opus" a measurement rather than a vibe (D15, and §6's 5× ratio), and the only place the false-negative rate becomes visible. Scoring one number instead of two would hide the exact trade D7 and D16 are balanced on. Needs real messages with expected tiers.
LThe four row verbs (D20) — desktop deep links, make-an-Inbox-item, not-replying, the tier dropdown, and a keyboard convention for all four Where the module stops being a report. Carries the message: scheme verification and the rfc822msgid angle-bracket normalisation (§3.3), and the fact that Make an Inbox item clears the row — the third drain path, without which the queue never empties (D18). The keybindings are proposed here rather than inherited: no row-level hotkey convention exists in the app yet, so this story is setting one for everything that follows. The tier dropdown is the write half of story N.
MTelegram push on ASAP (D16) Small — one fetch from the Worker against a channel the notifications spike already designed. Independent of everything except J producing a tier. Two suppressions to honour: backfilled messages (D21) and unjudged ones (D25) never push.
NThe corrections log as example set (D11) — comm_corrections, automatic promotion to few-shot examples, set versioning, pruning What turns a static rubric into one that improves. Depends on J for a prompt to inject into and L for the dropdown that produces a correction. Its interesting decision is how a set version's membership stays reconstructable after a prune (§8 Q4).
OReply detection (D18) — Gmail thread state, is_from_me on iMessage, the drain The slice that makes the queue self-clearing, and the one most likely to be subtly wrong: "replied" is not the same as "the thread moved", and a wrong answer either strands items or clears them silently. Deserves its own story precisely because it is small and dangerous.
PPer-account health (D17) — the daemon heartbeat, last_seen_at per account, the erroring state for a rejected Gmail token or a refused IMAP login, the header indicators and the banners Small and independent, but it is what makes a zero trustworthy. Without it the module's central claim is unverifiable from the surface. Note it is not only about the Mac: a green dot over an account whose token was revoked (D23) is the same failure wearing a friendlier face, and it is the one this story exists to make impossible.

A → B → C ships a real, inspectable module with no LLM and no Mac component in the repo at all. D makes it real for two accounts. E → F → G is the Mac chapter and can be paused without stranding anything — and G is the one slice whose value has an expiry date (D9, §8 Q6). H → I → J is the feature. K, M, O and P are independent of each other and can be ordered by appetite; N wants L first.

8 · Open questions

Revision 1's seven questions are settled and folded into §2. Q1–Q5 are what the second round left open. Q6–Q8 were opened by revision 3's review: each is a place where this document's own logic points somewhere the owner has not yet decided to go, and inventing an answer would be worse than naming the tension.

  1. What is the unit of the queue — a message, or a thread? A five-message email thread could produce five queued items or one. For a response queue the thread is arguably right: the owner owes a reply to a conversation, not to each message in it. But then which message's ask is shown, and what happens when a later message changes the tier — does the thread get promoted, and does that re-open something already cleared? Steve's is message-shaped because his is an inbox view. This is the largest unanswered design question in the epic, it touches comm_messages' shape, and it should be settled before story B fixes the schema.
  2. How are group chats handled? A busy iMessage group produces many messages, almost none of which ask anything of the owner specifically — but some do, and being named in one is exactly the obligation that gets missed. Triaging every message floods the classifier; triaging none loses the case that matters. Related to the threading question but not the same: a group's obligation is about addressing, not conversation shape. Settle before story F.
  3. Does the message: URL scheme actually work? Undocumented, widely used, and asserted in §3.3 on this document's authority alone. Story L should verify it on the owner's macOS version before building the affordance around it — and have the rfc822msgid: Gmail form as the fallback if it doesn't. The iOS half of §3.3 carries a second untested claim: that the same scheme opens a message there provided Mail has already downloaded it. The MVP doesn't ship mobile links, so nothing depends on it today — but the table says it, so someone should check it before a later story leans on it.
  4. How is "example-set version N" reconstructed after a prune? The bookkeeping half of this is already settled and shouldn't be re-litigated: §3.1 makes comm_corrections append-only, and a pruned example is flagged, not deleted, so the record that it happened always survives. What is genuinely open is that the flag is a mutable column on an append-only table: a verdict stamped "set v7" must still be answerable months later, and that needs the membership of v7 to be recoverable — as a snapshot, an explicit membership table, or a derivation from timestamps. Story N picks one; the cost of picking wrong is that "why did it say that" stops being answerable, which is the whole reason D11 versions anything.
  5. What exactly counts as "replied" for the drain? A thread advancing is not the same as the owner answering — a third party replying to all moves the thread without discharging anything, and Gmail's own thread state won't distinguish them. Getting this wrong strands items or, worse, clears them silently. Story O owns it, and it is the reason that story is separate.
  6. Does a channel with months left, not years, warrant story G at all? D9 now carries the dates: Amazon WorkMail takes no new customers after 2026-04-30 and shuts down entirely on 2027-03-31 — under seven months from this document's date. Story G is a whole IMAP client, its own auth and its own failure modes, for a mailbox that may well be gone before the epic finishes. Three live options: build it anyway (the daemon exists, the marginal cost is low, and the mailbox is real mail today); skip it and forward WorkMail into Gmail, which D17 already names as the escape hatch and which would also lift that account out of the Mac's sleep window; or wait for the migration and write the IMAP client once, against whatever replaces WorkMail. The third is more attractive than it looks, because the replacement will almost certainly speak IMAP too — so the daemon work is deferred, not wasted, whichever way this goes. The owner's "keep it on the Mac for now" is compatible with all three; what's undecided is when now ends. Settle before story G, not during it.
  7. Does Whenever need an escalation rule — and if not, why does the argument against snooze not also apply to it? §5 rejects snooze because it is "a machine for deferring things while feeling productive". Whenever is visible but inert: it doesn't age, escalate, resurface, or ever ask again. A message can sit there for three weeks — which D4 explicitly identifies as "the three-week-avoidance bucket" the tier was created to capture. The spec is therefore applying a stricter standard to the feature it rejects than to the one it keeps: snooze is condemned for enabling deferral, while Whenever is a tier that institutionalises it. This is not an argument for snooze — that decision is settled and stays settled. It is a question about the other half: whether an item that has been Whenever for N weeks should age into Today, appear in a periodic sweep, or be left exactly as it is on the grounds that visible-and-inert still beats invisible. Aging would collide with D19's "nothing is silently re-judged", which is itself a reason to think carefully rather than a reason to dismiss the question.
  8. Does the FYI shelf need a forcing function? D6 now admits what it can and can't claim: storing everything makes a false negative recoverable, but nothing makes the recovery happen. The shelf is unbadged (settled — and it must stay unbadged, or it becomes a second queue and the module has two inboxes again), uncounted, always large, and offered to an owner selected by an avoidance pattern. Story K's fixtures measure recall on a sample, which is the honest partial answer; the open part is whether anything should routinely put a few shelved messages in front of the owner — a small periodic sample surfaced in the existing weekly review, say — or whether an unforced audit is simply the accepted residual risk of D3. Answering "nothing" is a legitimate answer; leaving it unasked is what D6 was doing before.
