ALF-7 — Communication Firewall Module

Epic spec Durable context for the epic. Read this before refining any story inside it.

The one-line shape. A new Comms module aggregates four accounts — two Gmail, one Amazon WorkMail, and iMessage — and shows the owner one question: what do I owe someone a reply to? A small model reads each arriving message against a hand-written rubric and a list of important people, and assigns it to one of four tiers. Only messages that ask something of the owner are queued; everything else falls to an unbadged FYI shelf. alfred never becomes the store of record — every message stays where it already lives, and the module is a view over it.

Invariants every story honours. The decisions below are all binding, but these five are the ones a story cannot quietly break without breaking the module. If a story spec contradicts one of these, the story spec is wrong.

  1. Three exits from the queue, and only three (D18): the owner replies in their own client and detection clears the row; Nothing to answer / Not replying; or Make an Inbox item, which clears the row at that moment. No story may add a fourth, and none may ship a source or a surface that honours fewer.
  2. A message alfred cannot decode is stored and flagged, never skipped (D24). A skipped message is a false negative that leaves no trace anywhere in alfred.
  3. Nothing reaches ASAP that alfred did not judge ASAP on its merits (D21, D25). Backfilled and unjudged rows land no higher than Today.
  4. No verdict is ever silently re-judged (D19). Editing the rubric, the example set or the roster sweeps nothing; a re-run is an explicit action on an explicit row.
  5. alfred is never the store of record (D2). No story introduces state that exists only in alfred and would be a real loss, and nothing writes back to any source (D18).

1 · Problem space & why now

SPEC.md has named this module since the beginning: §13.2 sketches "an LLM with access to email / messaging … triages incoming messages by priority and urgency", §5.1 reserves a top-level tab, §5.2 the (firewall) route group, and the accent is already assigned — Firewall = blue (--color-accent-blue).

What the owner is actually buying

This shapes every decision below, and it is not what that one-line sketch implies. The prior art is Steve Newman's "Radar" (transcript at docs/communication-firewall/), whose return was volume-driven: a few hundred messages a day across five apps, checked thirty times a day, buying back hours. The owner's volume is roughly 15 texts and 30 emails on a heavy day — an order of magnitude less, so the time saved is maybe fifteen minutes, against which a rubric hand-tuned exception by exception is a real recurring cost (§6 carries the stop condition). Time is not the win. In the owner's words:

What it does buy is clarity and fewer items in my working memory — it lets me abstract "what do I need to respond to" to the single bit of "check comms". I'm afraid of (continuing to) put off or miss important communications because I get overwhelmed by triaging all of it. I'm fixing an avoidance pattern.

Three things follow, and they are why this epic looks different from the SPEC's sketch:

  • The product is a response queue, not a unified inbox. A message that asks nothing of the owner has no business in a queue whose whole purpose is answering "do I owe anyone anything."
  • A small, honest count is the mechanism — and zero is its resting state, not its normal one. "Check comms" collapses to one glance only if the number is small and moving. Two things keep it there, and neither is the classifier being stingy: only obligations enter at all (D3), and replying clears the row without a second act of bookkeeping (D18). A count that is routinely large, or that only ever grows, is the module failing. And Today is where the pressure lands: D5's floor pushes owed-but-undated work up into it, D7 resolves uncertainty down into it, and D25's two can't-judge paths deposit into it. The small-count claim has never been re-derived against that combined load — so if one tier turns out too large to read, it will be this one, and §6's stop condition is where that becomes visible.
  • A false negative is the failure that ends the module. One genuinely missed message doesn't cost that message — it costs trust, and the owner resumes checking Gmail "just in case", leaving two inboxes and a worse problem than before.

The three failures, ranked — because they collide

Those bullets name three different ways this module fails, and several decisions below buy one down by paying in another. D25 is the collision in its clearest form. So they are ranked here, once, and every decision that trades between them cites this order:

  1. A missed obligation — a real ask that never reaches the queue. Terminal: it costs trust rather than a message, and there is no recovery inside alfred once the owner has stopped believing the count. Every other failure is worth paying to avoid this one.
  2. A spurious ASAP. ASAP is the only tier that claims break focus for this, and that claim is spent once. A handful of unwarranted ones and the owner stops treating it differently from Today — at which point the module has no way left to say "now", and failure 2 has quietly become failure 1.
  3. A count that is routinely large, or that only ever grows. The glance stops working and "check comms" stops collapsing to one bit. Serious, but visible and tunable — it is what §6's stop condition watches, and it is the currency the other two are bought with.

Read D7 and D25 against that order and they stop looking like separate judgment calls: both spend 3 to avoid 1, and both refuse to spend 2 (uncertainty resolves into the queue, and never into a push).

What this epic builds on

  • ALF-158 (LLM processing) — the Inbox classifier established the pattern this epic reuses: a cron'd Worker sweep, a small model, an enum'd structured verdict, a service-role write, a per-failure degradation table, and a corrections-as-few-shot loop whose selection mechanics carry over even though its prompt and its content do not (D11).
  • The iMessage ingestion spike — settles that iMessage has no cloud API, that chat.db polling on a Mac is the only viable read path, and how to do it without the reliability gremlins.

2 · Decisions

What the module is

D1Comms is its own module with its own tables. A message is never an item.

New route group, new tab, new Supabase tables. Messages do not enter the generic-item core, and nothing auto-converts a message into a task or a code item.

Why: the lifecycles don't match. An item is captured, classified, filed and completed; a message arrives, is answered elsewhere, and is never "done" in alfred's sense. SPEC sizes the item core for hundreds of active items — email volume would drown the Inbox and break every existing count and view. The owner also wants spin-off to be a deliberate act: "If tasks/code spring off from communications, I'll do that manually."

D2alfred mirrors. It is never the store of record.

Every message stays in Gmail or Messages.app. alfred holds a copy, a verdict, and triage state. Losing alfred's database loses the interface and nothing else.

Why: this is the load-bearing property of Steve's system and the reason he can ship to production with no staging — "all my messages really live where they've always been… if this totally falls down, everything is still where it's always been. I just lose the nice interface to it." It also means retention and deletion in alfred are safe by construction.

D3Only messages that ask something of the owner enter the queuerevised

Obligation is the entry ticket, not a sort key. A message alfred judges to need no reply is never queued — it lands on the FYI shelf (D6).

The entry rule is about judgments, not about messages, and the difference is the whole of D25: what alfred could not judge at all is queued rather than shelved, because unknown is not nothing. So D3 reads "no obligation found means no queue", and D25 names the two ways a message can reach the queue with no finding behind it. A story that implements D3 as "no obligation means no queue" ships the false negative both decisions exist to prevent.

Why: the premise is avoidance, not time. The bit the owner wants is "do I owe anyone anything", and a queue containing no-action mail cannot answer it. Non-actionable mail is already handled fine in Gmail and Messages directly.

D4Four tiers: ASAP / Today / Whenever / FYI.

TierMeaningBadge
ASAPBreak focus for it. Someone is blocked on the owner, it is time-critical, or the sender is important enough that delay costs something real.Counted
TodayNeeds a reply before the owner logs off; not worth interrupting for. Most real correspondence.Counted
WheneverA reply is genuinely owed, but nothing turns on the date.Counted
FYIEverything else. No reply owed.None — collapsed by default

Why the shape diverges from Steve's: for Steve, the bottom tier is largely the junk pile. Here junk never enters the queue at all, so Whenever means owed but undated — which is precisely the three-week-avoidance bucket. It has to stay visible rather than being where things go to die.

D5One blended priority, with obligation as a floor.

The model returns a single tier, not a grid. Whether the owner owes a reply is an input to that one number — but an obligation never falls below Whenever.

Why: the owner asked for one number to glance at, and a 2×2 urgency × obligation grid gives four. But a pure urgency sort buries the non-urgent-but-owed message at the bottom — reintroducing the exact failure the epic exists to fix. The floor rule keeps one number while making that class of message structurally un-buriable.

D6Everything is stored, and FYI is the shelf it lands onrevised

Every ingested message is persisted and searchable regardless of tier, for 60 days (D27). FYI is an unbadged, collapsed shelf the owner can open to spot-check the rubric.

Why: once only obligations are queued (D3), a misjudged message is invisible rather than merely buried — a strictly worse failure mode. Storing everything keeps the evidence of a false negative in the building — for a bounded window, since D27 sweeps at 60 days — and FYI is the only surface on which the rubric's recall can be audited. Unbadged so it reads as an archive, not a to-do.

What it does not buy: a false negative becomes recoverable, not recovered. The shelf is unbadged, uncounted and always large by design, and offered to an owner selected by an avoidance pattern — so "open FYI and check" asks for the exact voluntary triage the module exists to remove, and nothing here forces it (§8 Q8).

D7Recall over precision — at the queue boundary, not at the ASAP boundaryrevised

The rubric is tuned to over-surface, and false negatives are the metric that decides in the evaluation fixtures. The bias has a direction as well as a size: uncertainty resolves into the queue and down to Today — queued rather than shelved, and never ASAP.

Why the asymmetry: one missed obligation costs the module its entire reason to exist. A message wrongly queued as Today costs a glance — a rounding error at ~45 messages a day.

Why the direction: a spurious ASAP doesn't cost a glance. ASAP is the only tier that claims break focus for this, so a spurious one spends the tier's credibility — and a tier the owner no longer trusts is the exact thing this module exists to prevent. Recall is therefore bought at the two boundaries where it is cheap (is this an obligation at all; Today or Whenever) and not at the one where it isn't. Story K scores them separately: recall on "did it reach the queue", precision on "did it reach ASAP".

What over-surfacing actually costs — stated correctly, because the obvious answer is wrong. A wrongly-queued message asks nothing, so no reply is ever sent, so reply detection (D18) never fires and the row never drains on its own. A false positive is not "one more row that clears itself"; it is a row that sits on Today until the owner opens it, confirms it asks nothing, and clears it by hand — the in-alfred bookkeeping step D18 identifies as the one an avoidance pattern skips, re-imported through the recall bias. That is §1's failure 3 being spent to buy down failure 1, which is the right way round, but it is a real price and not a rounding error.

Which is why the clearing gesture has to teach. The cheapest exit for a false positive is also the most common one, so if it records nothing, the example set (D11) starves exactly where D7 guarantees the corrections are richest. D20 settles that: clearing a row because it asked nothing is a demotion correction, one click, distinct from choosing not to answer something that did ask.

Why the bound still holds: the recall bias is bought at the two boundaries where the price is failure 3 and never at the one where it is failure 2 — so the count grows and the phone stays quiet, which is the only version of this trade §1's ranking permits.

Ingestion

D8The Mac daemon is a dumb pipe. All triage runs in the Worker.

The daemon polls, normalizes, and POSTs the full message to an authenticated alfred ingest endpoint (D22 settles what "authenticated" means). It contains no model call, holds no Anthropic key, and holds no Supabase credential.

Why: one classifier and one rubric across all four accounts. The alternative — triaging locally and shipping only a verdict — means two rubrics that drift apart, a second Anthropic key on a laptop when workers/src/index.ts (lines 32–33) says the Worker's is "the only place in the whole system that holds one", and a Comms view that can't show the message it's asking about. Accepted cost: personal iMessage bodies, including other people's words, are copied into Supabase and cross the internet to get there. Single-user project, TLS in transit, RLS on, the owner's own instance — weighed and accepted. D22 is what makes "authenticated" more than a word, and D27 says how long the copy lives.

WorkMail is Amazon's Exchange-compatible service and its practical read path is IMAP; since the daemon exists anyway, it rides along at near-zero marginal cost. Accepted cost: the Mac is a single point of failure for two of four sources.

Not because IMAP can't run in a Worker. An earlier revision claimed that and overstated it: Workers can open raw TCP via cloudflare:sockets and clients written for that runtime exist. What is true is only that nothing in this repo has tried one. WorkMail sits on the Mac because the daemon is already being built; moving it into the Worker later is a live option, not a rewrite.

A dated constraint, because it changes what story G is worth. AWS has published an end of support for Amazon WorkMail: no new customers after 2026-04-30, service ends 2027-03-31 — stated as dates rather than months-remaining because this document is never archived. So the owner's "keep WorkMail on the Mac for now" is load-bearing: the channel is temporary, though whatever replaces it will almost certainly speak IMAP too, so the daemon's client outlives the account. Whether the channel warrants story G at all is §8 Q6.

D10The daemon polls. It does not watch the filesystem.

Poll on last-seen ROWID, every few seconds, with a read-only SQLite connection. Wrapped in a launchd agent with a health check that fails loudly.

Why: straight from the spike. macOS coalesces FSEvents for background processes it considers idle, so a pure watcher silently stalls for minutes on a quiet machine — exactly the condition under which a firewall must keep working. Read-only avoids contention with Messages.app, which holds the write lock. The loud health check exists because Full Disk Access grants have been observed to reset silently across macOS updates, and a firewall that fails quietly is worse than one that fails.

D11The prompt carries a hand-edited rubric and a curated example set. Both are versionedrevised

Two artifacts, both editable in alfred, both stamped on every verdict:

  • A rubric — prose policy the owner can state outright. "Anything from my wife is ASAP."
  • An example set — corrected messages with their right answer, fed as few-shot examples. Every correction made through D20's tier dropdown becomes an example automatically; the owner can prune one that steers badly.

Why both: prose states the policy you can articulate; examples teach the judgment you can't. "This colleague writes in a way that sounds urgent but never is" does not survive being written down as a rule. Versioning both and stamping the verdict with both is what keeps "why did it say that" answerable — un-auditability comes from not knowing which examples were in a given prompt, not from having examples. Corrections do not replace policy: the rubric stays primary and examples refine its edges.

The example set is windowed, capped and direction-balanced — all three carried over from ALF-158's selectExamples (workers/src/prompt.ts). Which window, cap and balance exactly is story N's; that the draw is bounded and balanced is settled here, and so is a per-example length budget, because an example carries a denormalised email body (D27) and not ALF-158's short capture — see §6, where the cap and the truncation are the difference between the bill being real and being decorative.

Why bounded and balanced: ALF-158's D16 warns that a log dominated by one direction teaches the model to move that way. The case is stronger here, not weaker. D7 deliberately over-surfaces, so the corrections the owner actually makes will skew toward demotions — "you queued this, I'd have shelved it". An unbounded, unbalanced set therefore teaches the classifier to shelve more, eroding D7's recall bias and D3's entry rule through the very mechanism built to improve them, and doing it silently, since a wrongly-shelved message is the failure nobody sees (D6). The cap is load-bearing once more: §6's bill is only true of a prompt that stops growing. It is not load-bearing for caching — an earlier revision said an unbounded set "crosses Haiku's cache floor, where the cost arithmetic changes sign", which garbles the point. Crossing the 4,096-token floor only makes caching possible; nothing is cached without an explicit cache_control breakpoint, so a prompt over the floor and a prompt under it cost the same until someone asks for caching. §6 says why nobody should.

Revised from revision 1, which rejected few-shot examples outright — conflating replacing the rubric with augmenting it.

D12The people list is structured data, keyed on a personrevised

A contacts table — a person, their priority, and their many handles (a phone number, three email addresses). Rendered into the prompt; edited in the UI, not in prose.

Why: "these people are important" is a lookup, not a judgment. Keeping it out of the prose rubric makes it editable in one place and auditable ("flagged because it's from Dana"). Keying on the person rather than the address is forced by the multi-channel design: the same human is a phone number in iMessage and an email address in two mailboxes.

What structure does not buy. A roster is not immune to drift; it drifts silently, which is worse than prose, which is at least visible when next read. A new colleague, a changed number or a second address gets no floor-rule protection and nothing announces the gap — a false negative arriving through the one input the owner believed was exact. The mitigation: the roster must be editable from the message that was mistiered, not only from a settings page. Where that affordance sits is story H's (the §4 mockup deliberately doesn't draw it), and even then the trigger is the owner noticing.

D13Newsletters never reach the classifierrevised

Detected deterministically from headers — List-Unsubscribe, List-ID, Precedence: bulk — before any model call, and filed straight to FYI. They are reserved for the future Reader module (SPEC §13.1).

Why: bulk mail is always non-urgent and never an obligation, so a model call to decide that is pure waste. Steve runs Radar and his RSS reader as separate tools for the same reason. Headers answer it for free, and keeping the whale out of the prompt keeps both the counts and the bill honest.

Three conditions on the filter, all binding on story D:

  1. Strong signals only. A genuine list header — List-Unsubscribe or List-ID — not a lone Precedence: bulk, which plenty of ordinary automated mail sets.
  2. Record that the filter, not the model, shelved it (§3.1 carries the flag). A filtered message has no verdict and no reason, and must be distinguishable on the shelf from mail the model actually judged.
  3. Never filter a sender in comm_people (D12). A priority person on a mailing list is still a priority person.

Why those three, because these headers are conventions and not guarantees. Big ESPs set them; smaller senders and plenty of transactional mail do not, so the filter leaks both ways — unequally. A newsletter slipping through costs one Haiku call and lands on FYI with a verdict, self-correcting as the example set learns the shape. A real message wrongly filtered is the expensive one: it never reaches the classifier, so it sits on the shelf indistinguishable from mail the model judged — §1's failure 1 arriving through a deterministic path, where no amount of tuning the rubric can reach it.

D21Every source starts from a bounded lookback window, never from zero and never from "now"revised

Each account carries its own cursor on comm_accounts — a chat.db ROWID, an IMAP UIDVALIDITY+UID, a Gmail historyId. On first run it is seeded from a 7-day lookback; anything older is never ingested.

Why the window is decided here: both defaults a story would reach for are wrong, in opposite directions. Starting at zero classifies years of chat.db history — a bill in the tens or hundreds against §6's $3.30, and a queue full of obligations that died in 2023. Starting at max silently drops everything that arrived before the first tick: a false negative on day one, delivered by the system built to prevent them. A week is long enough that nothing live is missed at handover and short enough that the backfill is a rounding error.

The re-seed anchor

A re-seed anchors on last_seen_at when the cursor has one, and on now − 7d only when it doesn't — last_seen_at ?? (now − 7d), a fallback and not a max. The window is the first-run seed and nothing else. Cursors do get lost — a UIDVALIDITY change, an expired Gmail historyId (good for about a week), an account re-authorized after a lapse — and the account's own last successful poll is the only honest place to resume from.

The worked example, because the formula is easy to get backwards. Take D23's Testing-status refresh token dying on day 7, noticed and fixed on day 17. last_seen_at is day 7, so the re-seed anchors on day 7 and the ten missing days are ingested. Anchoring on max(last_seen_at, now − 7d) would pick the later of the two — max(day 7, day 10) = day 10 — and days 7–10 would never be ingested at all, which is precisely the gap this rule exists to close. max selects the window on every outage longer than the window, i.e. in exactly the case that matters.

The trade, named because it makes this a different decision from the one it replaces: after a long outage the window is no longer a cost bound — the catch-up is as large as the outage, and a month-long lapse means a month of backfill and a month's worth of Haiku calls. That is the right way round under §1's ranking: an unexpected bill is failure 3 and recoverable, a silently missing week is failure 1 and is not. D6's shelf cannot recover what never entered the building.

Why the worked example stays in the document: this rule has already been written down backwards once. The prose and the formula can disagree while both look right, and the version that reads most natural — "take the more recent anchor" — is the broken one.

The dedupe key is not the cursor

Settled here because it is schema (story B), and because the obvious cursor is a dangerous key for two of three sources:

SourceIdentity keyNever
Gmail ×2Gmail's message idhistoryId
WorkMail / IMAPRFC822 Message-ID (§3.1 captures it anyway), or (UIDVALIDITY, UID)bare UID
iMessagechat.db's guidROWID

Why: a cursor says where to resume; a key says whether two rows are the same message. A UIDVALIDITY change reassigns every IMAP UID, so keying on it either duplicates the mailbox or swallows a new message as a duplicate of an unrelated old one. chat.db ROWIDs are unstable across a rebuild, a restore, or a Messages-in-iCloud re-sync — a live condition per the spike. With the keys above a re-seed is a no-op; with the cursor as the key it is a corruption that looks like normal operation.

Two smaller rules that ride along. Backfilled messages are classified but never placed in ASAP, and that suppression keys on the message's age and the first sweep after a source is enabled rather than a "backfilled" flag, since any lag between a source starting and the classifier catching up makes the same backlog. And historical backfill beyond the window is out of scope — a deliberate, separate act if ever wanted.

D22The daemon signs the raw body with HMAC — a deliberate departure from the repo's bearer-key pattern, not an application of itrevised

The ingest endpoint verifies a sha256=… signature over the raw body, with a timestamp inside the signed payload so a captured request can't be replayed later to resurrect old messages. Comms gets its own secret, shared with nothing else. (Where the secret lives on the Mac is story E's: the keychain, never a launchd plist, never a dotfile, never this repo.)

What this does not buy, said plainly because an earlier revision claimed it: a stolen laptop. The daemon holds the HMAC secret exactly as it would hold a scoped bearer key, so a machine in someone else's hands can sign requests either way. That the credential can only write messages — no Supabase key, no Anthropic key — is D8's scoping, and a bearer key scoped the same way would give the same property. HMAC is not a laptop-theft control and this document should not be read as claiming one.

What it does buy is narrow and real: a captured signature is single-use and bound to this exact body. A bearer token replays forever and authenticates nothing about what it arrived with; a timestamped signature over the raw body does both. That matters only against someone on the path — TLS interception, or the header turning up in a proxy log — which is the threat model, and it is a smaller one than "stolen laptop". It is bought because D8 accepts that other people's private words cross the internet to reach this endpoint, and that is the traffic worth over-protecting.

The honest "why", because an earlier revision had it backwards. The repo's established pattern for a trusted external POST is a static bearer key, not HMAC: validateApiKey in frontend/lib/api/auth.ts takes x-api-key or Authorization: Bearer, resolveIngestClient turns a valid key into an admin client that bypasses RLS, and SPEC §7 prescribes exactly that for external ingress. The repo's HMAC code exists because GitHub dictates the signature format for its webhooks — a vendor requirement, not a house convention. So this decision departs from precedent and has to earn it on the narrow ground above and nothing wider. The cost is real and named: a second auth scheme in a repo that has one, implemented on both ends, for a threat that requires an adversary on the network path. A reviewer who thinks that trade is not worth it should reopen this decision rather than assume it was reasoned harder than it was.

What this does not fix: where the endpoint runs. §3.1 leaves "Worker route" versus "Next.js route handler" to story Q, and the check has to work either way — the Worker already has a constant-time verifier; a Next.js handler needs the same twenty lines against Web Crypto. Verify before parsing, whichever it is.

D23The Gmail OAuth client ships in "In production", not "Testing".

Before story D ships, the Google Cloud OAuth client's publishing status must be In production. Its refresh tokens are then stored as Worker secrets, one per account.

Why this is a named constraint and not a footnote: a client left in Testing issues refresh tokens that expire after 7 days, with no warning — killing both Gmail accounts a week after story D ships. Those are the two sources that carry the entire ASAP latency promise (D17), since the other two ride a Mac that sleeps. The one reliability guarantee this module makes would fail quietly, seven days in, for a reason that has nothing to do with the code.

The rest of the lifecycle, so it isn't rediscovered: gmail.readonly is a restricted scope, so an unverified production client shows an "unverified app" screen on first consent — a warning to click through, not a blocker, for a personal client. And even a production token can die (a password change, a revoke, six months of non-use), so the failure is rarer, not impossible — which is why D17's health surface covers Gmail and not just the Mac.

D24A message alfred can't decode is stored and flagged. It is never skipped.

A failed attributedBody decode, an odd MIME part, an unknown encoding: the row is still written, with whatever body was extracted (possibly empty) and a flag saying extraction failed. The poll loop logs it and moves on — one bad row never stalls ingestion.

Why "skip it" is not available: a skipped message is a false negative by construction, and the worst kind — it leaves no trace anywhere in alfred, so not even the FYI shelf (D6) can be audited into finding it. Storing a row with an empty body costs nothing and keeps the failure visible. What happens to it then is D25's: it is never judged FYI on the strength of text that failed to extract, so it takes the can't-judge path into the queue, marked, where Open in source (D20) is the recovery — the message still lives in its own client, which is what D2 guarantees.

The verdict

D14The verdict states the ask, not the topic.

The per-message summary answers "what does this want from me, and by when" — not "what is this about". "Dana needs the invoice approved before Friday", never "regarding the Q3 invoice".

Why: what makes triage aversive is ambiguity about whether a message contains an obligation. A topic summary preserves that ambiguity and forces the owner to open the message to resolve it — which is the exact work the module is supposed to remove.

D15Haiku 4.5 to start, chosen by evaluation rather than by price. One call per message.

About $3.30/month on Haiku 4.5 at the owner's volume, against $16.50 on Opus 5 — a flat 5×, since Opus is 5× on both sides (§6, which names its caching assumption rather than assuming a warm cache, and which says what the example set adds on top of both). A real ratio on a small absolute number: the eval fixtures still decide the model (story K), but if Opus wins there the cost is worth re-reading rather than waving through as noise.

Why one call per message: batching ten messages per call would save roughly $1.50/month and cost ALF-158's per-item isolation, where one malformed verdict damages exactly one row. At this volume that is a bad trade. The Batches API's 50% discount is a trap here — it is asynchronous with up to 24-hour turnaround, and a firewall that reports the urgent thing tomorrow is not a firewall.

D25A message alfred could not judge enters the queue at Today, marked. Nothing is ever silently shelved.revised

Two things put a message in the queue with no judgment behind it, and they get one rule rather than two: a body that wouldn't decode (D24) and no verdict that will ever come (content-shaped failures exhausted the ceiling, below). Both land on Today, flagged, with an ask that says which. Counted, and never ASAP — a tier alfred didn't choose doesn't get the one that claims "now". A re-run (D19) helps only once something has changed: a different model, an edited rubric, a later vision path — never as a retry of the identical call.

A message with no readable text is classified, not bypassed

A photo-only iMessage, an attachment with an empty body: the classifier still runs. It is handed [image attachment, not read] in place of the body, plus the sender and the D12 roster, and priority does its job:

  • Priority person + attachment → Today, flagged. "Sign this" with a photo of a form comes from someone who matters, and that is the case worth protecting.
  • Anyone else → FYI, flagged — visibly, so the shelf shows that alfred knew there was something it could not read.

Why this stopped being a bypass: an earlier revision sent every text-less message straight to Today and defended it with "if any is more than a rarity that is a bug upstream". Photo-only messages are not a rarity on a personal phone and are not a bug — they are most of what a phone carries. Each one landed on a counted tier, could not drain by reply (nobody owes a reply to a photo of a dog), and needed the owner to open it and clear it by hand: the voluntary triage this module exists to remove, re-imported through the safety path. The sharp case — "sign this" with a photo of a form — is a case about who sent it, and D12 already answers that, so the priority roster carries the rule instead of a blanket exception. D6's false-negative-recoverable property is preserved by the flag; what is no longer paid is §1's failure 3 on every ordinary day.

Why the remaining two get one rule: the alternative in both cases is invisibility — §1's failure 1 arriving through the machinery meant to prevent it, and worse than a mistiered message because there is no verdict to audit. Both are also genuinely rare, which is what the discarded attachment case was not: a decode failure is a bug in the daemon and five content-shaped failures in a row is a bug in the prompt. If either becomes routine, that is a defect to fix upstream and not a tier to get used to.

The tension with D3, stated rather than buried: neither has been shown to be an obligation. They are admitted because unknown is not nothing — D7's direction is that uncertainty resolves into the queue and down to Today, and total uncertainty is still uncertainty.

Which failures count toward the ceiling

One stateless request per message, no repair loop, ceiling of five attempts (the reused sweep's). What counts against it is where this epic diverges from ALF-158, deliberately:

What went wrongWhat the sweep does
Transport — 429, 5xx, timeout, network Nothing written, nothing counted. The message stays an ordinary unclassified row; the next sweep is the retry. No queue, no backoff state.
Systemic — binding absent, a rejected credential (401/403), or a 400 on a request shape identical for every message The tick aborts; nothing written, counted or marked, so fixing the deploy later loses nothing. Ingestion keeps running (D6): this is a classifier outage, and D17's health surface says so in those words.
Content-shaped — unparseable, a tier outside the enum, max_tokens Attempt counted, no re-ask — the same prompt mostly produces the same shape. At five, the message takes the path above.
stop_reason: "refusal" Terminal, its own state — below. Not the can't-judge path.

Why transport must not count, spelled out because the reused code counts it. workers/src/sweep.ts calls countAttempt on every failure including transport — "Counting uniformly is what gives the ceiling teeth" — with a ceiling of five and a two-minute cron. That is safe in ALF-158 because its ceiling is inert: the item goes on being an ordinary unclassified Inbox row and the worst case equals today. Here the ceiling is active, and uniform counting plus an active ceiling is a flood — a sustained 429/5xx window burns all five attempts for every eligible message in about ten minutes, so a twenty-minute Anthropic incident deposits the entire inbound stream into Today, newsletters that leaked D13 included, none of it drainable by replying (D18) because nobody owes a reply. That is exactly the "count that only ever grows" §1 names as the module failing.

Refusals go to the shelf, flagged — not into the queue

A stop_reason: "refusal" is terminal and gets its own state. The message is filed FYI with a visible refused flag, distinguishable from "asked and answered no" exactly as D13's filtered mail is. It is not the can't-judge path, it does not enter the queue, and it does not count toward the ceiling.

Why the shelf and not the queue: re-sending an identical prompt cannot change a refusal, so D19's re-run is no recovery; and refusals skew toward the spam and phishing that already leaked D13's filter, so queueing them would spend the module's premise on its least valuable mail. The cost: a refusal over a genuine obligation is §1's failure 1, whose only defence is a flag on a shelf nobody is forced to open — the partial answer D6 gives and §8 Q8 is open about. Refusals being rare is what makes this right; if they aren't, the volume will say so.

Scope note: the MVP does not send images to the model (D15). The flag is the honest minimum and it makes the upgrade targetable — a later story can route exactly those rows to a vision call without re-classifying anything else (D19).

Living with the constraints

D17No source is assumed to be working. The module says so on its face, per accountrevised

Every account carries its own health: last_seen_at, stamped on every successful poll — not on every message — plus the last error and when it happened. The header shows one indicator per account, in three states:

Thresholds are per source: Gmail rides the Worker's cron and should be minutes fresh; the Mac's two are expected to go dark overnight. The §4 mockup draws it.

Why per-account, and why "erroring" is its own state: a revoked token or a rate-limited account would otherwise leave a dot green while nothing at all was arriving — a surface that looks empty, current and trustworthy over two dead sources. "Quiet" and "broken" are indistinguishable from message volume alone; only a successful poll separates them, which is why the heartbeat is stamped on the poll and not the mail. A firewall's value is that its zero means something (§1), and a zero that can silently mean "we stopped looking" is worth less than none.

The heartbeat's cadence is decided here, not in the story that draws the indicators. Liveness coalesces: the daemon reports it at most once a minute, including when there is nothing to send. That makes a zero-message heartbeat a first-class payload of the ingest endpoint — part of story Q's contract, not a shape story P bolts on later. The Worker's Gmail poll stamps last_seen_at directly on its own cron and needs no heartbeat.

Why coalesced: the daemon polls every few seconds (D10), so one heartbeat per poll would be tens of thousands of requests a day to move an indicator whose thresholds are in minutes.

The consequence, easy to discover late: an ASAP iMessage arriving at 11pm reaches the owner when the Mac next wakes. The ASAP latency promise holds for the two Gmail accounts only. WorkMail inherits the limit — kept there for now (D9) rather than forwarded into Gmail, which remains the escape hatch.

Read-only in both directions: no labels, no archive, no state pushed back to any source. Instead alfred watches. When the owner replies in their own client, the item clears itself. Every source needs its own detector, and each one is part of that source's story — a source without one honours two of the three exits below, which is not enough:

Why this beats write-back, which was the obvious design: Steve writes a Gmail label so his two inboxes don't diverge. Detection achieves the same convergence without alfred touching the mailbox, and removes the second bookkeeping step entirely — the step an avoidance pattern skips. A design where the owner must mark things handled inside alfred would fail in the specific way this epic exists to prevent.

There are exactly three ways a row leaves the queue, and every story must honour all three:

  1. The owner replies in their own client → detection clears it. The main path, and the only one that requires nothing of the owner inside alfred.
  2. Nothing to answer / Not replying (D20) — the two halves of the manual clear, one for a row that shouldn't have been queued and one for a conscious decision to leave a real ask unanswered. The fallback, since detection cannot clear a reply that never comes.
  3. Make an Inbox item (D20) — the obligation moves into the Inbox, which becomes its durable tracker, and the comms row clears at that moment.

Why the third is not optional: without it, spinning a message into a task is a way out of the message and not out of the queue — two trackers for one obligation, which is the duplicated-inbox failure §1 and D1 are organised against, showing up as the one symptom this module cannot afford: a count that never reaches zero for reasons the owner can't act on.

D19Re-classification is on demand only. Nothing is silently re-judged.

Editing the rubric or the example set does not sweep the existing queue. A re-run is an explicit action.

Why: this is ALF-158's "the machine changed my answer back" failure. A verdict the owner has already seen — and possibly already acted on — moving underneath them costs more trust than a stale tier costs attention.

Deliberately absent — quick reply and forward: each needs send capability, SMTP or AppleScript, which nothing else here requires — a real scope expansion for a convenience the owner's clients already cover.

D27Messages expire after 60 days. Examples don't. A deletion at the source does not propagaterevised

A scheduled sweep deletes every comm_messages row older than 60 days — the whole row, body included, across every tier. The example set is exempt: a correction keeps its denormalised text indefinitely, and examples are not swept. A manual purge — one message, one account, or everything older than a chosen date — still ships with the schema in story B, and unlike the sweep it does cascade into the example set: the deliberate "I want this gone" action has to actually work, or it is theatre.

A message deleted in Gmail, unsent in Messages, or removed from a mailbox stays in alfred until the sweep reaches it; deleting something in alfred never touches the source (D18).

Why 60 days, and why the owner chose it: the driver is security, not cost. Volume never forces the question — ~16,000 rows a year is nothing — so retention here buys exactly one thing: a bounded blast radius if this database is ever exposed. D2 only goes one way, alfred's copy is safe to lose and not safe to keep, and a 60-day window is the owner minimising what there is to lose. The number is a comfort judgment, not a derived one, and nothing in the design depends on its exact value.

Why examples are exempt: a bounded, versioned handful of corrections is a risk the owner has weighed and accepted, and it is the artifact that makes D11's set improve rather than reset every two months. An earlier revision argued the opposite on the grounds that "the most-corrected messages are the most engaged-with" — that is false and has been removed. A correction is a tier fix; it says nothing about how much of a message the owner read, and does not mark it as more personal than any other.

What 60 days costs, stated so no story session is surprised:

  • A still-owed obligation can be deleted. Whenever is the owed-but-undated tier — Q7 exists because it has no aging rule, and §4 draws a row there at three weeks as ordinary. The sweep is blanket by decision, so a Whenever row that outlives 60 days is deleted while still owed. That is failure 1 (§1), caused by housekeeping rather than by the classifier. Exempting rows still in the queue is a single predicate on the sweep and would remove this entirely; the owner has been shown the trade and chose the blanket rule. Whether Q7's escalation rule closes it instead is the cleaner long-term answer.
  • alfred's search becomes a 60-day search. Anything older is a Gmail search — a real narrowing of a benefit D8's dumb pipe partly rested on.
  • D6's shelf audit gets a horizon. A false negative is recoverable for 60 days, not forever — defensible, since a miss is noticed well inside that or not at all.
  • Story K's fixtures must be snapshotted out before they expire, or the eval corpus is a rolling two months rather than a growing one.

D28iMessage edits and unsends are invisible to alfred. Accepted, and stated.

The daemon polls forward on ROWID (D10, D21), which sees inserts. An edit rewrites an existing row in place and an unsend blanks or removes one — neither allocates a new ROWID, so a cursor-based poll never revisits either. So: an edited message is triaged on its original wording, forever, and an unsent message persists in alfred's mirror until the 60-day sweep reaches it (D27), possibly still asking for a reply to something the sender retracted.

Why accept rather than fix: catching either needs a full-table diff on every poll — throwing away the cursor that makes polling cheap and safe — or dylib injection into Messages.app, an explicit non-goal (§5) that costs SIP. Neither is worth it for a triage tool, and D19 means the fix wouldn't be automatic anyway.

Why it is survivable: D2. Open in source (D20) is the arbiter — if a row's ask reads oddly, the source is one click away and is always the truth. The failure here is a stale row, which is visible and correctable; it is not a missing one.

Two ingestion homes, one classifier. The daemon carries no model call and no key.

One call per message, structured output, every field enum'd or short. Following ALF-158's verdict shape:

{

5 · Constraints & non-goals

  • No calendar half. Steve's Radar is half filtered-calendar with per-event shortcuts — a different data source and a different UI.
  • No automatic task or code creation. Spin-off is a deliberate human act, but a one-keystroke one (D20).
  • No snooze. Every tool of this shape has one, and it is rejected on purpose: a snooze button is a machine for deferring things while feeling productive, in a module built to fix an avoidance pattern. If Today becomes a tier the owner ignores wholesale, revisit — that would be evidence the pressure valve was load-bearing. (§8 Q7 records the asymmetry this creates with Whenever, which is kept and is also inert.)
  • No sending — no quick reply, no forward (D20); both need SMTP or AppleScript into Messages.app, which nothing else here requires. And no write-back to any source (D18): alfred reads, and never labels, archives or modifies a mailbox.
  • No newsletter reading, summarizing or digesting. Bulk mail is detected and shelved (D13); making it useful is the Reader module's job (SPEC §13.1).
  • No WhatsApp, Slack, Signal or Twitter. Steve ingests all of them; this epic covers the owner's four actual channels.
  • No voicemail integration. iOS Visual Voicemail is programmatically inaccessible; the path, if wanted, is a carrier setting that emails the transcript — at which point it is simply another email.
  • No dylib injection into Messages.app. Read receipts and reactions are reachable that way, at the cost of disabling SIP. Read-only plus AppleScript is the ceiling.
  • No reading of images or attachments. One text call per message (D15); a message whose ask is inside a photo is flagged and classified on its sender, not interpreted (D25). Routing those rows to a vision call is a later story the flag exists to make possible.
  • No deletion sync (D27) — a source-side delete is not mirrored; the 60-day sweep is the only thing that removes a message, and alfred never writes a deletion back. And no historical backfill: each source starts from a 7-day window (D21).
  • alfred is never the store of record (D2) — no story may introduce state that exists only in alfred and would be a real loss.
  • Ingestion freshness is bounded by the Mac. While it sleeps, iMessage and WorkMail stop arriving; the module says so visibly rather than looking empty and current (D17).

6 · What it costs to run

Haiku 4.5 at $1.00/MTok input, $5.00/MTok output. Each call carries roughly 1,500 tokens of instructions, rubric and rendered people list, plus the message body, plus ~130 tokens of verdict.

~$0.0022 (iMessage) / ~$0.0026 (email)~$0.11~$3.30
Those figures exclude the example set, and the example set is not small. $0.0022 is 1,500 input tokens and ~130 output — the prefix and a short text, with no room in it for anything else. An example denormalises the message text (D27), and here that text is an email body, not ALF-158's one-line capture: budget ~200–400 tokens per example untruncated. A dozen of those is 2,400–4,800 tokens — more than the entire prefix above, and enough on its own to double or triple the bill and to carry a "capped" prompt across Haiku's 4,096-token cache floor. So story N owes two numbers, not one: how many examples and how much of each message survives into one. A cap of n examples truncated to ~150 tokens keeps the table above roughly honest (~+$1.50/month); a cap of n full bodies does not.

Even so, the table is only true of a prompt that stops growing — which is why D11 caps the set at all. An uncapped set grows the prefix monotonically, and "worst case" quietly becomes "day one".

Cross-check against ALF-158. Its table puts the Inbox classifier at ~$1.20 per month at 20/day; this epic's per-call rate over the same 20 gives ~$1.44. The two reconcile — but not because this prompt carries an example set that ALF-158's doesn't (its D16 is titled for exactly that, and workers/src/prompt.ts ships the selection code). The gap is a units mismatch: ALF-158's ~1.5k is its whole input, the 1,500 above is only the prefix, and the difference is the message body — which the Inbox's short captures largely sit inside and email does not.

Opus 5 and caching, the short version. Opus is 5× Haiku on both sides, so at this volume it is $16.50/month against Haiku's $3.30 — a real ratio on a small absolute number, so D15 stands and story K's fixtures decide the model on eval, not on price. Caching does not rescue the comparison and must not be planned around: Haiku 4.5's minimum cacheable prefix is 4,096 tokens (Opus 5's is 512), and crossing that floor only makes caching possible — nothing is cached without an explicit cache_control breakpoint, so a prompt that crosses it by accident costs exactly what it did before. Chasing it deliberately is the mistake ALF-158's D16 documents: a write costs 1.25× against a read's 0.1×, and on bursty traffic where the write expires before anything reads it, caching is a ~25% loss. Every figure above assumes no caching, and that is a decision, not an omission.

The other cost: upkeep, and when to stop paying it

§1 names the real risk of this epic — at fifteen minutes of triage saved a day, a rubric hand-tuned exception by exception can cost more than it returns. And the module ships three separately-tunable artifacts: the rubric (D11), the example set (D11), and the people roster (D12). No target rate is set here, because any number would be invented and unfalsifiable. What is set is the condition for stopping.

The stop condition, stated so it can actually fire: if, after six weeks of real traffic with the correction gesture available, the correction rate is not falling, or the owner is editing the rubric more than weekly to chase the same class of mistake, then the tuning loop is not converging and more rubric is not the answer. The moves at that point are a better model (story K makes that a measurement, not a vibe), fewer sources, or a coarser split — and if none of them work, the honest reading is that this module costs more than the fifteen minutes it saves, which §1 already admits is the thing to watch.

The anchor is traffic, not a story number — an earlier revision said "six weeks after story J", and under the build order J precedes both D (the first real message) and L (the verbs that record a correction). At J+6 weeks there is nothing to measure and the condition cannot fire. So the clock starts at the first full week in which real messages are being classified and the owner can record a correction — in practice the week after L, and never before D.

What the correction rate cannot see, and it is the failure §1 ranks first. Two ALF-158 sentences were carried over here and neither survives the transplant:
  • "Every wrong guess becomes a correction." True in ALF-158, where every guess sits on a row the owner reviews. Here a false negative is a verdict on the FYI shelf that nothing forces open (D6, §8 Q8), so it never becomes a correction at all. The correction rate therefore measures the demotion rate — §1's failure 3, the cheap one — and is structurally blind to failure 1.
  • "Recorded automatically by work the owner was doing anyway." Also not true here. A correction is a deliberate gesture on a row — Nothing to answer, or the tier dropdown (D20) — not a diff of edits the owner was making regardless. A falling correction rate is as consistent with the owner correcting less as with the classifier improving.
So the stop condition is a convergence check, not an accuracy metric, and it needs a companion that can see recall: story K's fixtures, re-run rather than run once, are the only instrument in this epic that ever measures a false negative. If Q8 is answered with a periodic FYI sample, that becomes the second one.

The build order

A → B → C → H → I → J → L → D → O → Q → E → F → G, with K, M, N and P placed by appetite.

  • A → B → C ships a real, inspectable module with no LLM and no Mac component in the repo at all. B needs Q1's default confirmed and nothing else.
  • H → I → J comes next, before any real source — and the reason is not the one an earlier revision gave. It said C "builds four tiers and a set of flags, and none of them means not classified yet", which is false twice over: D25's transport row leaves an unclassified message as an ordinary stored row by design, and C builds the classifier-stalled banner precisely to say "nothing is being judged". The real reason is about the product, not the schema: ingestion without a classifier is a unified inbox — the thing §1 says this module is not — and the owner's first impression of Comms would be a list of untriaged mail. Nothing is lost by waiting, because J needs H and I anyway.
  • L before D, which is the correction that matters most. D's first tick backfills seven days (D21) into a queue with no exit of any kind until L and no main exit until O. Built the other way round, the owner's first impression of the module is a week of accumulated obligations that cannot be cleared — §1's failure 3 delivered as a welcome screen. L is buildable against C's seeded data, so this costs nothing.
  • D → O closely: D18 says every story must honour all three exits, and until O the main one doesn't exist, so between D and O the count still only grows. That gap should be days, not weeks.
  • Q → E → F → G is the Mac chapter and can be paused without stranding anything. Q lands immediately before E because the daemon is its only client. O's is_from_me half follows F, and WorkMail's drain is inside G — until those ship, D18's first exit exists for Gmail alone, which is a stated limitation and not an oversight. G is the one slice whose value has an expiry date (D9, §8 Q6).
  • K, M, N, P are independent. N wants L first; M and P want at least one live source; K wants J and is worth doing early, since §6 now leans on it as the only instrument that can see a false negative.

8 · Open questions

Revision 1's seven questions are settled and folded into §2. Q1–Q5 are what the second round left open; Q6–Q8 are places where this document's own logic points somewhere the owner has not decided to go, and inventing an answer would be worse than naming the tension. Q0 is new, sits above all of them, and is the owner's to answer.

Q0 — Are the owner's notifications staying on?

The top open question, and the one this document has never asked. Steve Newman silenced five apps and checked Radar instead; that is what made his a firewall rather than a report. Nothing in this epic asks whether the owner will silence Gmail and Messages.app — and the answer changes what several settled decisions are worth.

If notifications stay on, nothing is fire-walled. An iMessage buzzes the phone, the owner reads and triages it there, and alfred — bounded by a sleeping Mac (D17) — records it afterwards. The module becomes a retrospective ledger of unanswered texts, which is a real but much smaller product than §1 describes.

If notifications go off, two things get sharper at once. The Mac-asleep window stops being a footnote and becomes a hole the owner is relying on not to exist, so D17's health surface carries far more weight than its placement in the build order suggests. And because this epic decides no mechanism by which alfred reaches the owner at all, the only way to learn an ASAP row exists is to open the module and look — which is the voluntary habit the adjacent gap below is about. Whether alfred should be able to reach out, and how, is undecided and deliberately not settled here.

Two adjacent gaps, recorded with it because they have the same shape:

  • The count is never surfaced outside the module. No tab badge is decided anywhere in this document and none is drawn in §4 — the word "badge" appears only as unbadged. So "check comms" is a new voluntary habit, asked of someone §1 describes as selected by an avoidance pattern. Whether the module gets a badge on its tab is a real decision this epic has never taken.
  • alfred is a third read-only surface, not a replacement for two. D18 sends the owner into Gmail or Messages to reply, every time, by design. That is the right call for write-back; it does mean the owner now has three places to look rather than one.

This re-ranks D17, D25 and Q7, and it is deliberately not answered here — a guess would propagate into every story that renders a count. It is not an argument for splitting the epic; that question is already recorded at the end of this section.

  1. What is the unit of the queue — a message, or a thread? A five-message email thread could produce five queued items or one. For a response queue the thread is arguably right: the owner owes a reply to a conversation, not to each message. But then which message's ask is shown, and what happens when a later message changes the tier — does the thread get promoted, and does that re-open something already cleared? The largest unanswered design question in the epic, and it touches comm_messages' shape.

    Proposed default, so story B is not blocked on an absent answer: the unit is the message, and comm_messages carries a thread key from the first migration (§3.1 already lists one). Rolling up to one row per thread then becomes a query and a view — a change to C and O, not a migration — while starting at the thread and discovering the ask needs per-message granularity is the expensive direction. It also matches D18: reply detection reads thread state either way, so the drain works under both.

    Who confirms: the owner, in story B's own refinement session, before the migration is written — not in a separate conversation that may never happen. If they say nothing, the default above is what ships, and this is the paragraph a later reader should point at.

  2. How are group chats handled? A busy iMessage group produces many messages, almost none of which ask anything of the owner specifically — but some do, and being named in one is exactly the obligation that gets missed. Triaging every message floods the classifier; triaging none loses the case that matters. Related to threading but not the same: a group's obligation is about addressing, not conversation shape. Settle before story F.
  3. How does a row actually open its message? Apple Mail's message: scheme is undocumented and widely reported to work; Gmail's rfc822msgid: operator is the fallback and treats the header's angle brackets differently; iMessage can only open the conversation. This epic asserts none of it — earlier revisions did, in build detail they simultaneously disclaimed. What is settled is that the RFC822 Message-ID is captured for every email from story B onward (§3.1), so the schemes are a UI question and never a backfill. Story L verifies before it builds, on the owner's actual macOS version.
  4. How is "example-set version N" reconstructed after a prune? The bookkeeping half is settled: comm_corrections is append-only and a pruned example is flagged, not deleted. What is open is that the flag is a mutable column on an append-only table — a verdict stamped "set v7" must still be answerable months later, which needs v7's membership recoverable as a snapshot, an explicit membership table, or a derivation from timestamps. Story N picks one; picking wrong means "why did it say that" stops being answerable, which is the whole reason D11 versions anything. D27's purge cascade lands on the same table and should be designed with it.
  5. What exactly counts as "replied" for the drain? A thread advancing is not the same as the owner answering — a third party replying to all moves the thread without discharging anything, and Gmail's thread state won't distinguish them. Getting this wrong strands items or, worse, clears them silently. Story O owns it, and it is the reason that story is separate.
  6. Does a channel with months left, not years, warrant story G at all? D9 carries the dates — no new WorkMail customers after 2026-04-30, service ends 2027-03-31 — and whoever reads this should work out the remaining life against those rather than trust a figure baked in on the day it was written. Story G is a whole IMAP client, its own auth and its own failure modes, for a mailbox that may be gone before the epic finishes. Three live options: build it anyway (the daemon exists, the mailbox is real mail today); skip it and forward WorkMail into Gmail, which D17 already names as the escape hatch and which would lift that account out of the Mac's sleep window; or wait and write the IMAP client once, against whatever replaces WorkMail — more attractive than it looks, since the replacement will almost certainly speak IMAP too, so the work is deferred rather than wasted. The owner's "for now" is compatible with all three; what's undecided is when now ends. Settle before story G, not during it.
  7. Does Whenever need an escalation rule — and if not, why doesn't the argument against snooze apply to it? §5 rejects snooze as "a machine for deferring things while feeling productive". Whenever is visible but inert: it doesn't age, escalate, resurface or ever ask again. Both §4 mockups draw the same three-week-old row — sitting exactly as it arrived on the bad day, and still sitting there in the resting state, which is what makes the resting state non-zero. That is the span D4 identifies as the avoidance bucket the tier exists to capture. So the spec applies a stricter standard to the feature it rejects than to the one it keeps. This is not an argument for snooze, which stays settled. It is a question about the other half: whether an item that has been Whenever for N weeks should age into Today, appear in a periodic sweep, or be left exactly as it is on the grounds that visible-and-inert still beats invisible. Aging would collide with D19, which is a reason to think carefully rather than to dismiss the question.
  8. Does the FYI shelf need a forcing function? D6 admits what it can and can't claim: storing everything makes a false negative recoverable, but nothing makes the recovery happen. The shelf is unbadged (settled — badge it and it becomes a second queue and the module has two inboxes again), uncounted, always large, and offered to an owner selected by an avoidance pattern. Story K's fixtures measure recall on a sample, which is the honest partial answer; the open part is whether anything should routinely put a few shelved messages in front of the owner — a small periodic sample in the existing weekly review, say. D25 adds two more tenants: a refused message is filed here with a visible flag, and so is a text-less message from anyone not on the priority roster — and a flag only helps someone who looks. Answering "nothing" is legitimate; leaving it unasked is what D6 was doing before.
Raised, not decided — is this one epic? Recorded here so it isn't lost, and flagged as the owner's call, not this document's. Seventeen stories spanning a Cloudflare Worker, a Next.js module, a Supabase schema and a shipped macOS daemon is arguably a program rather than an epic. The E → F → G Mac chapter in particular has its own deployment target, its own credential scheme, its own permission model, its own failure modes, and — in G — one slice with an expiry date; almost nothing about it is shared with the rest beyond the ingest contract in story B. Against that: every slice serves the one question in §1, and splitting would give two documents that each re-derive the same premise, which is the outcome an epic spec exists to prevent. Nothing in this document has been split in anticipation of an answer.
