ALF-280 — Podcasts — and any YouTube video — in the Reader

Make YouTube the Reader’s video source, not podcast RSS. The Mac daemon fetches existing captions with a self-updating yt-dlp, and the Worker summarises them with timestamps that jump back into the video.

Where we landed

Treat a video like a newsletter post. A pasted link becomes a video Inbox item, and Dispatch turns it into a queued Reader post. ALF-298’s research items already follow exactly this route. The daemon pulls the queue, fetches the captions on the owner’s home internet connection, and posts them back. The existing summariser then writes the gist and overview, with each idea pinned to a moment in the video. Channel subscriptions come second: they feed the same queue and skip the Inbox. Next, cut two stories: (1) ad-hoc video items end to end, then (2) channel subscriptions.

  1. Source

    YouTube covers podcasts and every other video; RSS covers only podcasts.

    None of the three target shows publishes a transcript in its feed — §2

  2. Transcripts

    Fetch captions that already exist instead of transcribing audio: $0 instead of ~$0.30 an episode.

    Speech-to-text vendors priced in §3

  3. Where it runs

    The Mac daemon, because YouTube blocks cloud IPs and its official captions API only works for videos you own.

    The owner picked owning the fetcher over a captions middleman — §4

  4. Keeping yt-dlp alive

    Standalone binary, a daily --update-to nightly, and Node as the JavaScript runtime it needs.

    Since 2025.11.12, YouTube downloads need an external JS runtime — §5.3

  5. Cost

    About $0.09 an episode, all of it the Claude summary; roughly $2 a month.

    Sonnet 5 on a ~30k-token transcript — §7

The evidence

Everything below is for whoever builds on this. Acting on the answer needs only what’s above.

How the question moved

The ticket asked for podcasts in the Reader: ingest new episodes, transcribe them or find transcripts, and timestamp the key ideas so a section can be heard without the whole episode. It also asked about platforms, ingestion, and where transcription runs and at what cost. Interviewing the owner reshaped it three times:

  • The owner doesn’t listen to podcasts. Episodes are too long and too thin for their schedule. Friends send YouTube links, and every show the owner cares about is on YouTube. The goal is to skim a whole medium the owner currently skips.
  • Both ad-hoc and subscription intake, eventually. Ad-hoc comes first as the MVP. The end state is “point a show at alfred and let it surface the good bits”.
  • No personalisation. “Surface the relevant points” means the Reader’s existing static highlighting (ALF-232 D7), not ranking against an interests profile.

About half of the links friends send are YouTube-only (talks, lectures, interviews with no podcast feed). That decided it: YouTube, not RSS, is the one source that serves both intake paths and any video at all.

The epic’s non-goal. ALF-232 §5 lists “no podcasts” and “email via the comms mirror is the only intake”. Two later stories already broke that rule on purpose: Instapaper (ALF-272, migration 0041) and research reports (ALF-298, 0047). Both added a reader_posts.source value with its own identity branch. A YouTube source is the third instance of that pattern. A refinement should say explicitly that it supersedes §5.

  • The Argument match was the Audioboom feed by “The Argument”, not NYT Opinion’s show of the same name. That’s assumed to be the right one.

Where the text can come from

Every way to get a timed transcript of a video or episode, priced for the owner’s volume: three shows is about 20 episodes a month, roughly 1.5 h each, around 30 audio-hours a month, plus ad-hoc links. The owner’s point was that $10 a month would still be more than double alfred’s whole current API bill, and that ruled out the top of the table. Cost doesn’t decide between the bottom options, though. YouTube does: only a residential IP, or a middleman, can fetch the captions.

Why the Mac, and what that costs

YouTube blocks cloud IPs. The youtube-transcript-api README states that requests from AWS, GCP, Azure and similar providers mostly fail with RequestBlocked/IpBlocked. Cloudflare’s Worker egress counts as one of those. The daemon runs on the owner’s home connection.

The owner weighed it against a captions middleman and chose to own the fetcher. yt-dlp is still a dependency, but it comes with no account, no bill and no vendor; the risk is whether its maintainers keep pace with YouTube, which they have so far. Speed doesn’t matter (“these things are by definition not urgent”), so a laptop that’s asleep only delays a post.

The real cost is a design change, not money. daemon/README.md describes the daemon as a write-only dumb pipe: it holds one ingest credential, posts messages, and never reads anything back. This feature needs it to receive work, meaning to learn which videos to fetch. The proposal (§5.3) keeps that as narrow as possible: a signed GET that returns only public video IDs. The refinement has to decide that trust change deliberately.

Terms of Service. Both yt-dlp and every captions middleman break YouTube’s ToS, which forbids automated access outside the official API. For one person’s reading list the practical risk is negligible, and the owner has been told.

Technical shape

Detailed enough for a refinement to expand; the refinement decides the names.

5.1 Ad-hoc intake: a video item type, modelled on ALF-298

The owner pastes the link into the Inbox (their choice) and it waits for Dispatch (also their choice). That keeps one Inbox model, and a link can still be a plain “watch this” task. ALF-298’s research item is the template to copy (docs/specs/archive/ALF-298.html, migrations 0046–0047):

  • Enum value in its own migration file. alter type item_type add value 'video' alone, because Postgres refuses to use a new enum value inside the transaction that added it. 0046’s header records that trap.
  • Typing without a model call. A capture whose text is a YouTube URL (youtube.com/watch?v=, youtu.be/, /shorts/, /live/) can be typed video by pattern before the classifier (workers/src/verdict.ts) runs. ALF-298 D7 lets the classifier guess research; this one is deterministic.
  • Dispatch. Copy the send_items_to_research RPC: stamp dispatched_at, insert a queued reader_posts row, and delete the item, all in one transaction. Also relax items_dispatched_needs_folder for video, and add the per-type branch through the dispatch path (row flags, the Classify menus, lib/tasks/dispatch.ts, the store’s dispatchItems). ALF-298’s file list covers it.

5.2 Data

  • reader_posts.source gains 'youtube', with a new branch in reader_posts_source_identity: youtube_video_id is required and none of the mail identity is set. A partial unique index on youtube_video_id where source = 'youtube' dedupes a link pasted twice against a subscription that picks up the same video.
  • A captions lifecycle column, by analogy with research_state: captions_state = queued | fetching | done | unavailable | failed, plus captions_attempts, captions_error and captions_fetched_at, and a derived “stale” with Retry, as in ALF-298 D12. unavailable (no captions yet) is different from failed (yt-dlp broke) because the two need different fixes.
  • The post body stores the transcript as timed segments, not the flat text a newsletter uses: [{start, end, text}] as JSON, plus duration_seconds, channel_id/channel_name and the creator chapters. The existing 90-day text sweep applies to it.
  • Subscriptions need a channel roster. reader_publications is keyed on an email handle, so a separate small reader_channels table (channel id, name, enabled) is probably cleaner than overloading it. That’s Story 2’s call.

5.3 The daemon’s new job: fetching captions

  • Pull. On its existing ~5-minute cadence (EXPECTED_INTERVAL_SECONDS = 300 in daemon/src/contract.ts), the daemon calls a new HMAC-signed Worker endpoint, for example GET /reader/videos/pending, which returns [{post_id, video_id}]. The endpoint flips each returned row to fetching with a lease, the way the reader tick’s summarizing_since lease works, so an overlapping run doesn’t double-fetch. Whether this reuses the comms ingest secret or gets its own is part of the trust decision in §4.
  • Fetch. Prefer creator captions over auto captions, and take JSON metadata in the same call: 
    yt-dlp --skip-download --write-subs --write-auto-subs \
  --sub-langs "en.*" --sub-format json3 \
  --dump-json --no-simulate --js-runtimes node:<path> \
  -o "<tmp>/%(id)s" "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=<id>"
    --dump-json supplies the title, channel, duration, description and chapters. Prefer json3 over VTT: auto-caption VTT is known to repeat rolling lines, which would need deduping. Verify both formats on real videos during the build.
  • Deliver. POST the segments and metadata back over the same signed path the comms ingest uses (workers/src/comms/ingest.ts). The Worker validates the body, writes it, and sets captions_state = done, after which the summariser picks the post up like any other. If no captions exist yet, report unavailable instead. Auto captions can lag hours behind an upload, so the queue retries with backoff and gives up after about 48 hours.
  • Keeping yt-dlp alive (an explicit requirement from the owner):
    • Install the standalone macOS binary into the daemon’s own support directory, not via Homebrew or pip. Only the standalone binary self-updates with -U; package-manager installs update through the package manager.
    • Run yt-dlp --update-to nightly at daemon start, once a day, and immediately after any extraction failure, then retry once. The nightly channel builds daily and gets YouTube fixes days before stable.
    • JS runtime: since 2025.11.12, yt-dlp needs an external JavaScript runtime to solve YouTube’s challenges. Deno is recommended; Node ≥ 20 is supported via --js-runtimes node:<path>, and the daemon already runs on Node. Pass the daemon’s own Node path so there’s nothing new to install. Deno remains an option if Node proves flaky.
    • Report the yt-dlp version and the last update result on the daemon’s heartbeat, so the Reader’s health surface can say “captions fetcher broken since Tuesday” instead of showing a queue that silently stalls.
  • PO tokens. YouTube may require proof-of-origin tokens for subtitle requests on some clients. yt-dlp added PO-token support for subtitles, and enforcement keeps changing (PO Token Guide). This is exactly the churn the auto-update exists for.

5.4 The summary: the Reader’s shape plus timestamps

  • The output is the same D6 shape (workers/src/reader/schema.ts: headline, gist, novel ideas, evidence, argument, who should read). It stays static: no interests profile, per the owner.
  • Timestamps on bullets. For a youtube post, each novel_ideas/evidence item becomes {text, at_seconds}. Use a source-specific schema variant rather than changing the newsletter schema that every post shares. As schema.ts already does with budgets, check at_seconds after parsing (it must be ≤ the duration) and snap it to the nearest segment start.
  • A segment map. Use the creator’s chapters when the video has them (all three target shows do), with a one-line summary for each. When it doesn’t, the model proposes 5–12 segments from the timed transcript.
  • Prompt input. Merge caption segments into ~30-second paragraphs, each prefixed [mm:ss], which keeps the token count near the spoken word count. A 2-hour episode comes to about 19k words, or 30–35k input tokens. That’s within Sonnet 5’s 1M context, but the existing 400k-character intake ceiling (ALF-232 D3) needs checking against a 2.5-hour transcript.
  • Links. Each timestamp is https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=<id>&t=<s>s, which opens the YouTube app at that moment on the phone. “Open original” is the plain video URL.

5.5 Subscriptions (Story 2)

  • Every channel has a public, key-free Atom feed, https://www.youtube.com/feeds/videos.xml?channel_id=<id>, which lists its 15 most recent uploads. It still worked in 2026, though it had intermittent 404s from Dec 2025, now stable. The reader tick polls each enabled channel and queues any unseen video_id directly as a post, skipping the Inbox, the way a newsletter arrives.
  • Unverified: whether YouTube serves that feed to Cloudflare’s IPs. If it doesn’t, the daemon polls the feeds too, and the channel list then has to be available to it.
  • The feed includes Shorts. Skip anything the daemon reports as under ~3 minutes.
  • Cost guard: videos count against the Reader’s daily model-call ceiling (READER_DAILY_CAP, ALF-232 D9). At about 3× an article’s cost per call, a separate small cap may read better. That’s the refinement’s call.

Alternatives we’re not taking

Podcast RSS feeds plus cloud speech-to-text

The first plan, and it works for all three shows: the MP3s are plainly downloadable, AssemblyAI takes a URL plus a webhook, and it costs ~$0.30 an episode. Ruled out because it covers only podcasts. Half of the owner’s ad-hoc links are YouTube-only, and the owner wants “any content on YouTube”. It also pays to transcribe audio when captions usually already exist.

A captions middleman (Supadata and others)

Fully cloud and likely free at this volume. The owner chose not to add an external dependency whose business is out-maneuvering YouTube. If the daemon route proves too brittle, this is the fallback, behind the same “captions fetcher” seam, with no changes needed to the Reader.

Workers AI Whisper or Groq in the cloud

The cheapest speech-to-text options ($0.03–0.04/h), but they need audio, and audio is exactly what YouTube won’t serve to a Worker. They’re only relevant to an RSS design.

Substack transcripts through the existing newsletter pipe

It looked like zero new intake, because the shows post to Substack and the Reader already ingests Substack mail. The Cognitive Revolution’s Substack page had no Transcript tab, and the player captions come from an undocumented endpoint.

Instapaper as the intake

Instapaper is already a Reader source (ALF-272) and saves YouTube links. The owner preferred pasting into the alfred Inbox, which also gets the Dispatch gate for free.

Personalised segment ranking

Raised because “surface the relevant points” could mean ranking against the owner’s interests. The owner clarified it means the existing static highlighting, so ALF-232 D7 stands.

Transcribing locally as the primary path

Briefly favoured on cost, before captions came up. It’s kept only as the fallback for a video with no captions (§7).

Cost & open questions

Money

  • Captions cost $0.
  • The summary on Sonnet 5 ($2 / $10 per M tokens) is about 30–35k tokens in and 2–3k out, roughly $0.09 for a 2-hour episode. At about 20 subscribed episodes plus about 10 ad-hoc videos a month, that’s about $2–3 a month, against about $10 a month for the RSS plus speech-to-text design.

Risks

  • The YouTube arms race. JS challenges, PO tokens and IP reputation keep changing, and yt-dlp is the buffer. Auto-update and health reporting turn a break into a visible stall rather than silent rot.
  • The daemon’s trust boundary moves (§4), and it needs a deliberate refinement decision.
  • Freshness depends on the Mac being awake. The owner accepted this.
  • Caption quality. Auto captions mishear jargon (MLST especially) and carry no speaker labels. A summary tolerates both better than a human reader would, but this hasn’t been tested on these shows.

Unverified: check in the build or refinement

  • Whether the Cloudflare Worker can fetch youtube.com/feeds/videos.xml. This sandbox can’t reach YouTube at all.
  • How often the target shows’ videos have captions, and how many hours auto captions lag after upload.
  • yt-dlp with --js-runtimes node on the daemon’s Node version, and the json3 output format, on a real video.
  • The real token count of a 2.5-hour transcript, against the 400k-character intake ceiling.
  • All vendor prices in §3. They came from search snippets; the official pages were blocked here.

Open for the refinement

  • Whether the pull endpoint gets its own secret or reuses the comms ingest secret.
  • A reader_channels table, or growing reader_publications.
  • A separate daily cap for videos.
  • When a video has no captions, whether to fall back to local Whisper or show “no transcript” with “open original”. The second is enough for an MVP.
