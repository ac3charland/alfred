ALF-334 — When is high effort worth it over medium?

Almost never on its own. Medium matched high on four of five stories. On the one it lost, a concurrency bug, high also failed: of five runs each, high shipped two and medium one.

Where we landed

Keep Opus 5.5 at medium for bugs and spec stories. Bump to high only for a ticket about ordering between several writers: optimistic UI, RPC replies and realtime echoes racing each other. Even then, don’t count on high: on ALF-250 it failed two runs of five, and medium failed four. What the passing runs shared wasn’t effort but design: one guard covering every writer that touched the rank. Two of the three also wrote a seeded fuzz test. Next I’d trial, on that class of ticket only, a launch-prompt clause asking for a fuzz test over the interleavings. It costs less than high effort and aims at what actually separated the runs.

The evidence

Everything below is for whoever builds on this. Acting on the answer needs only what’s above.

The experiment

The ticket set the design: Phase 1 ran medium against high on stories of rising difficulty until blind judges found one where medium wasn’t ready to ship and high was. Phase 2 reran that story to see whether the failure was consistent or sporadic. Before anything ran, the owner set three things:

  • Budget. $500+ for the arms at first, lowered to about $300 all-in once Phase 2 was nearly done. The spike finished at about $245.
  • Ladder. I picked it, weighted toward bugs: hidden causes, same-cause siblings and a misleading symptom, rather than size. ALF-312 had already shown a 108-file story tie, so size alone doesn’t separate the levels. Each rung is a bug whose merged fix has a behavioural regression test, so “ready to ship” has an objective check as well as judges.
  • Stop rule. If no rung diverged, stop and report.

Held equal across arms

  • Model and base. Opus 5.5 on both arms. Each arm starts from main at its original session’s start, plus one commit setting effortLevel in .claude/settings.json, the route ALF-312 verified. Every arm’s report includes CLAUDE_EFFORT and the per-entry "effort" counts from its transcripts, main and subagents. All of them ran at their pinned level.
  • Prompt. The original launch prompt verbatim, behind one shared preamble: push to alf-334/replay-*, no PR, nobody answers questions, don’t read the merged solution, report to the parent. ALF-277 and ALF-250 had owner answers mid-session that carried facts the agent needed: ALF-277’s console stack trace, transcribed from two screenshots, and ALF-250’s symptoms. Those went into the prompt up front for both arms. Procedural owner turns were dropped.
  • Environment. The same cloud environment for every arm.

Grading

  • Hidden tests. The merged fix’s regression tests, dropped into each arm and run through npm run test, or Playwright for ALF-216. Each was checked to fail on the base.
  • Blind judges. Patches masked and given neutral names, with images, demos and skill notes excluded. Phase 1 used pairwise judges: two families on the hard rung, each in both orders; on the easy rungs a screening pair of one Opus and one Sonnet in opposite orders. Phase 2 used one absolute Opus judge per patch, blind to level. The r1 arms were re-judged in that mode too, so all ten ALF-250 verdicts come from the same instrument.
  • Probe suite (ALF-250). Ten standalone end-state tests the Phase 1 judges wrote: after the interleaving settles, the store’s ranks equal the server’s, with no tie. Plus two transient checks. I ran all twelve against base and every arm.

Phase 1 hit: ALF-250 r1

Both r1 arms replaced the sentinel swap with one locked or single-write UPDATE and serialized the swap RPCs. They split on the client:

  • Medium. It skipped a reply whose priority equalled its prediction, and swallowed realtime echoes that matched a ledger of its own writes. It covered swaps only. A jump to top or bottom followed by a quick nudge ends in a permanent tie. Base heals that same sequence: the swap’s reply lands last.
  • High. It stamped every optimistic write, swaps and both jump kinds alike. It ignored server ranks for any row with an unanswered stamp, re-read ranks after a failure, and flushed queued writes on unmount.

  • Against the pre-set rule: consistent needs medium not ready in ≥ 4 of 5 and high shipping in ≥ 4 of 5. Medium cleared its half (4 of 5). High didn’t: it failed r3, r4 and r5. So the r1 split was sporadic. At this difficulty, neither level reliably ships in one unattended session.
  • The difference: medium 1 of 5 against high 2 of 5. One-sided Fisher exact, p ≈ 0.50. That’s a hint that high helps, not a result.
  • A judge inconsistency: medium r2 failed, and high r2 shipped, over the same class of defect (a lost echo followed by another device’s write). Different judges weighted the same rare case differently. Under one uniform rule, blocking only on a lasting defect a single tab can reach, medium ships 2 of 5 (r2, r5) and high 2 of 5 (r1, r2): no difference at all.
  • The probes are necessary but not sufficient: medium r4 passed 9 of 10 and still jammed its queue. The probes were written against r1’s flaws, so they can’t see a new failure class. A judge reading each patch is still needed.

What separated the shipping runs

I read every arm’s report and diff. The three that shipped (high r1, high r2, medium r5) share one trait. Every rank writer goes through one guard: nudges, top and bottom of Backlog, top and bottom of project, and the story modal’s controls. That guard was a stamp or token per optimistic write, or a single store-owned queue. Five of the seven failing runs left jumps outside it (medium r1 and r3, high r3, r4 and r5). Of the other two, medium r2 matched echoes by value with a ledger that leaks when an echo is lost, and medium r4 jammed its own queue on a promise bug. Two of the three passing runs also wrote a seeded fuzz test of the real list against a simulated server (high r2: 24 of 24 seeds failed on the old store; medium r5: 300 seeds swept). One failing run, medium r2, passed every probe and had no fuzz test. Its blocker needed a second device, which a single-tab fuzz wouldn’t model either.

Effort made that design more likely: two of five at high against one of five at medium. But it didn’t make it reliable. Both levels produced the swaps-only design (medium r1, high r3 and r5) and the full-coverage design (high r1 and r2, medium r5). The original session needed three review rounds, owner steering and a property test to get there.

ALF-307: a spec-built story

ALF-307 (PR #445) collapses FYI shelf threads into conversations. The original session had no owner turns, and its review caught a blocking gap in the spec itself: threads cut at the page edge (51f5595).

  • Both arms caught that same gap in their own review round and fixed it, knowingly deviating from the spec’s D7. Both pass the original’s completion tests (7 of 7).
  • Judges. Opus (A/B) and Sonnet (B/A) both say both arms ship, and both prefer high. High keeps a conversation open when its selected message is clicked shut, or when a message other than the oldest is promoted; that’s the D9 behaviour row. Medium collapses it in both cases. The original only fixed that after its PR (14ca72f).
  • Cost. Medium $9.22, high $15.57.

So on a spec story high bought one UX nuance a reviewer would catch, at 1.7× the cost.

Running replays: what broke

  • Auto mode can refuse git update-ref refs/remotes/origin/main, the preamble rule ALF-312 used. One arm in eight (ALF-314 high) was refused, couldn’t push, and correctly refused the parent’s suggested workaround. Later arms used git fetch origin <own base branch>:refs/remotes/origin/main, plus a fallback: paste the diff into the report. Its diff came back that way and was graded uncommitted.
  • Turbopack rejects a symlinked node_modules (“points out of the filesystem root”). Jest is fine with a symlink, but a Playwright run in a scratch worktree needs a real tree; cp -al hard links work.
  • A pushed base commit triggers the pre-push gate. Pushing an old main commit to a new branch name skips the package suites only if HEAD is on a docs-only branch. So I committed the draft findings first.
  • The usage limit. The 5-hour window stopped all ten in-flight arms at once. A send_message resume worked once it reset. Two containers then restarted mid-push and lost their background gate; they idle until the parent nudges them.
  • Judges sharing a scratch directory. One judge deleted another’s worktree with a glob. Give each judge a path containing its own id.

Alternatives we’re not taking

Make high the default “to be safe”

On four of five stories it bought nothing a judge would block on, at 1.2–2× the cost. On the fifth it failed two runs of five as well.

Climb further, to xhigh, on concurrency tickets

Untested. ALF-312 found xhigh bought breadth, not correctness. Here the failures came from design choices that both levels made. A process change that forces every interleaving to be tested (fuzz, a second review round) targets that more directly.

More reruns for a tighter interval

A 20-point gap needs about 80 runs per arm (ALF-283). The ledger trial gets those from ordinary launches, and the owner capped this spike at about $300.

Open questions

  • Does a launch-prompt clause asking for a seeded fuzz or property test lift concurrency tickets at medium? The correlation here is 2 of 3 passing runs against 0 of 7 failing ones, and it is untested as an intervention.
  • How noisy is a single absolute judge? Same-class defects drew different verdicts. A two-judge panel per patch, with a tiered rubric, would firm that up.
  • Refinement and spikes at medium are still unmeasured, as ALF-312 noted.
